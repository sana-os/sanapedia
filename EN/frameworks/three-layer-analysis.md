# Three-Layer Analysis

## 1. Purpose

Three-Layer Analysis is a structural reading framework for examining historical action through three analytical lenses:

* Layer A — Survival
* Layer B — Narrative
* Layer C — Institution

The framework is intended to prevent historical explanation from collapsing into a single cause.

It asks where pressures, constraints, expectations, and formal arrangements were operating, and how changes in one area may have created friction in another.

The three layers are not evidence themselves.

They are analytical lenses for reading historical evidence and scholarship.

---

## 2. The three layers

| Layer       | Name        | Primary concerns                                                                                    |
| ----------- | ----------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Layer A** | Survival    | Food, security, resources, climate, logistics, disease, warfare, population, movement               |
| **Layer B** | Narrative   | Religion, memory, legitimacy, identity, symbols, fear, hope, honour                                 |
| **Layer C** | Institution | Law, administration, taxation, military systems, offices, treaties, courts, education, formal rules |

The layers describe different kinds of structural conditions.

They may overlap.

They may reinforce one another.

They may also come into friction.

---

## 3. Layer A — Survival

Layer A concerns material, demographic, logistical, environmental, and security conditions that shape the range of available action.

Its primary concerns include:

* food;
* security;
* resources;
* climate;
* logistics;
* disease;
* warfare;
* population;
* movement.

Layer A asks whether the material and practical conditions of life were stable, strained, disrupted, or changing.

Relevant questions include:

* Were food or essential resources available?
* Were movement and logistics reliable?
* Did disease or demographic change alter available capacity?
* Did warfare or insecurity change the practical choices available?
* Were population movements placing pressure on existing arrangements?

Layer A does not claim that material conditions mechanically determine historical action.

It identifies pressures and constraints that may shape what actions are possible, costly, or sustainable.

---

## 4. Layer B — Narrative

Layer B concerns the meanings through which historical actors understood themselves, others, institutions, events, and possible action.

Its primary concerns include:

* religion;
* memory;
* legitimacy;
* identity;
* symbols;
* fear;
* hope;
* honour.

Narrative in this framework does not mean that a belief is fictional or unimportant.

It refers to the interpretive structures through which individuals and communities understand the world.

Relevant questions include:

* What memories shaped expectations?
* What claims to legitimacy enabled or constrained action?
* What identities or symbols organized belonging?
* What fears or hopes affected judgment?
* What forms of honour, faith, or inherited narrative made particular actions appear acceptable, necessary, or impossible?

Layer B is especially important when actions cannot be understood only through material incentives or formal rules.

---

## 5. Layer C — Institution

Layer C concerns formalized rules, organizations, offices, procedures, and systems.

Its primary concerns include:

* law;
* administration;
* taxation;
* military systems;
* offices;
* treaties;
* courts;
* education;
* formal rules.

Institutions can stabilize expectations and coordinate action.

They can also lag behind social conditions, become difficult to enforce, or come into conflict with lived experience.

Relevant questions include:

* What formal rules existed?
* Which institutions had authority?
* How were resources collected or distributed?
* How were offices and responsibilities organized?
* Which legal, military, administrative, or educational systems structured action?
* Did institutional arrangements match everyday conditions?
* Where did formal rules and lived experience diverge?

Layer C therefore examines not only the existence of institutions, but also their relationship to actual historical conditions.

---

## 6. The layers are not three causes

Three-Layer Analysis is not a claim that history can be explained through only three causes.

The layers are analytical categories.

They help organize questions.

A historical development may involve all three layers at the same time.

For example, a change in material conditions may interact with legitimacy, memory, or institutional rules.

An institutional reform may alter material distribution.

A narrative of legitimacy may help sustain an institution.

A crisis in one layer may increase pressure in another.

The framework therefore does not ask:

> Which single layer caused the event?

It asks instead:

> Where were the relevant pressures, constraints, expectations, and institutional arrangements, and how did they interact?

---

## 7. Structural load and friction

The framework is particularly useful for identifying where structural load accumulated.

When using Three-Layer Analysis, ask:

* Which layers were relatively stable?
* Where did pressure accumulate?
* How did change in one layer create friction in another?
* Where did institutional arrangements diverge from everyday experience?
* Which memories or claims to legitimacy enabled action?
* Which memories or claims to legitimacy constrained action?

The term structural load refers to accumulated pressure within or between the layers.

The framework does not assume that pressure automatically produces a particular result.

Similar pressures can produce different historical outcomes under different conditions.

The analytical task is therefore to identify:

* where pressure existed;
* how it was distributed;
* which relationships were strained;
* what remained stable;
* what changed;
* what constraints and possibilities followed.

---

## 8. Analytical lens, not evidence

Layer A / B / C are not evidence.

They are a way of organizing questions about evidence.

A claim that an institution was weak, a population was under material pressure, or legitimacy was contested still requires historical support.

The framework cannot establish such claims by itself.

Evidence may come from records, material remains, scholarship, comparative analysis, or other appropriate historical methods.

Three-Layer Analysis should therefore be used after or alongside source examination, not in place of it.

A useful analytical category does not make an unsupported claim true.

---

## 9. Relationship to Source Trifurcation

Source Trifurcation and Three-Layer Analysis answer different questions.

Source Trifurcation asks:

> What kind of historical information are we observing?

Its categories are:

* Fact
* Perception
* Record

Three-Layer Analysis asks:

> Where are structural pressures, narratives, and institutions operating?

Its categories are:

* Survival
* Narrative
* Institution

A Record may provide evidence relevant to any of the three layers.

A Perception may be especially relevant to Layer B, but it may also affect institutional behavior or responses to material conditions.

A Fact may concern developments in any layer.

The two frameworks therefore complement one another.

They should not be collapsed into a single classification system.

---

## 10. What Three-Layer Analysis does not claim

Three-Layer Analysis does not claim that:

* three layers are sufficient to explain all history;
* the layers are three universal causes;
* the layers operate independently;
* every historical case gives equal importance to all three layers;
* structural conditions determine one inevitable outcome;
* classification into a layer is itself evidence;
* institutional rules always reflect lived reality;
* narratives are less real historically because they concern belief or meaning;
* material conditions automatically override memory, legitimacy, or institutions.

The framework is a method for organizing observation and structural questions.

It is not a substitute for source criticism, chronology, comparative research, area knowledge, or historiographical debate.

---

## 11. Relationship to other methodology

Three-Layer Analysis is one part of the wider Pastfold historical-reading method.

It should be read together with:

* SANA Historical Reading Protocol — the broader reading and documentation standard;
* Source Trifurcation — separation of Fact, Perception, and Record;
* Revision and Citation Policy — preservation of provenance and epistemic status during revision and reuse;
* Article Specification — structural and metadata requirements for Pastfold knowledge pages.

These documents operate at different analytical layers.

Source Trifurcation asks:

> What kind of historical information are we looking at?

Three-Layer Analysis asks:

> Where are structural pressures, narratives, and institutions operating?

Revision and Citation Policy asks:

> What must remain traceable when the material is revised, cited, or transformed?

Article Specification asks:

> How is the knowledge object represented and maintained?

Keeping these responsibilities separate helps Pastfold distinguish evidence from analytical framework while still examining how material conditions, narratives, and institutions interact.
