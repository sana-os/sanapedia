# Source Trifurcation

## 1. Purpose

Source Trifurcation is a historical reading framework that separates three different objects of observation:

- Fact
- Perception
- Record

The purpose of the distinction is to prevent different kinds of historical information from being treated as though they were the same thing.

A surviving record is not automatically the event itself.

A belief held by historical actors is not automatically a factual description of the world.

A factual reconstruction is not independent of the records, material traces, and research through which it is investigated.

The distinction does not make historical inquiry less certain.

It makes the basis and limits of historical claims more visible.

---

## 2. Core distinction

| Category | Meaning | Main question |
|---|---|---|
| **Fact** | An event, condition, or relationship that may have existed in the past and can be investigated through evidence | What can be established, and to what degree? |
| **Perception** | How contemporaries or later communities understood an event, condition, person, institution, or relationship | What did people believe, fear, expect, remember, or regard as legitimate? |
| **Record** | What survives through documents, inscriptions, objects, archaeology, institutions, chronicles, archives, oral transmission, or later narratives | Who preserved what, when, for what purpose, and in what form? |

These categories are related.

They are not interchangeable.

---

## 3. Fact

Fact does not mean a perfectly recoverable past.

The past itself cannot be directly observed from the present.

Historical facts are investigated through surviving evidence, material traces, institutional remains, comparative research, and the convergence or divergence of records.

When presenting a factual claim, ask where possible:

- What can be established?
- What evidence supports it?
- How independent are the supporting records?
- What are the limits of the evidence?
- Is the chronology secure?
- Is the location secure?
- Is the identity or agency of the actors secure?
- Are important parts disputed?
- How confidently can the claim be stated?

A factual claim should not be stated with greater certainty than its evidence supports.

Fact therefore refers not only to what happened in the past, but also to the present research question of what can responsibly be established about it.

---

## 4. Perception

Perception is how people understood the world in which they acted.

It is not a category for dismissing historical beliefs as irrational, false, or biased.

People do not act only on information that later researchers can verify as fact.

Historical action may be shaped by:

- fear;
- faith;
- rumour;
- memory;
- honour;
- legitimacy;
- identity;
- belonging;
- expectations of protection;
- expectations of reward;
- anxiety about the future;
- inherited narratives.

A perception may be factually inaccurate and still be historically consequential.

When examining perception, the question is therefore not only:

> Was this belief factually correct?

It is also:

> For whom, under what conditions, did this understanding make a particular action appear reasonable, necessary, legitimate, dangerous, or possible?

Perception can influence cooperation, resistance, migration, conflict, religious action, institutional change, political loyalty, and the interpretation of later events.

Understanding a perception does not require agreeing with it.

---

## 5. Record

A record is not the past itself.

Records are surviving traces through which the past is investigated.

They may include:

- administrative documents;
- letters;
- chronicles;
- inscriptions;
- coins;
- legal texts;
- archaeological remains;
- architecture;
- images;
- maps;
- oral traditions;
- institutional records;
- later historical narratives.

Every record has conditions of production and survival.

When examining a record, ask:

- When was it created?
- Who created it?
- For whom was it created?
- Why was it created?
- What did it seek to justify, criticize, record, remember, classify, or preserve?
- What conventions shaped its form?
- Was it copied, edited, translated, excerpted, or reorganized?
- What survived?
- What did not survive?
- What may never have been recorded?
- How was it reused or reinterpreted later?

Survival itself can create historical distortion.

What remains available to researchers is not necessarily a representative sample of everything that once existed.

---

## 6. Why the categories must not collapse

Source Trifurcation is useful because the three categories can interact without becoming identical.

### Record is not Fact

A record can make a claim about an event without proving that the event occurred exactly as described.

Records must be compared with other records, material evidence, chronology, institutional context, and source conditions.

### Perception is not Fact

Historical actors may believe something that cannot be independently established.

That belief can still be historically important because people act within perceived realities as well as material ones.

### Record may preserve Perception

A letter, chronicle, law, ritual text, inscription, or later narrative may preserve evidence of what individuals or communities believed.

The record may therefore be evidence for perception even when it is weak evidence for the factual event it describes.

### Fact may be reconstructed from multiple Records

No single record necessarily provides a complete account.

Factual reconstruction may depend on convergence among different records, material traces, institutional evidence, and later research.

### Later Records may reshape earlier Perceptions

A later narrative can preserve earlier memory, reinterpret it, simplify it, or create new meanings.

Researchers must therefore distinguish between a contemporary perception and a later community's perception of the same past.

---

## 7. Relationship to evidence, interpretation, and hypothesis

Fact / Perception / Record and evidence / interpretation / hypothesis answer different questions.

Source Trifurcation asks:

> What kind of historical object are we observing?

Evidence-status asks:

> How strongly is a particular claim supported?

A Record can be securely dated while the claim contained inside it remains uncertain.

A Perception can be strongly evidenced even when the belief itself was factually inaccurate.

A proposed Fact may remain contested because surviving evidence is incomplete.

An Interpretation may compare several Facts, Perceptions, and Records.

A Hypothesis may propose a relationship among them that cannot yet be directly established.

Therefore:

- Fact does not automatically mean certain;
- Record does not automatically mean reliable;
- Perception does not automatically mean false;
- interpretation does not automatically mean fact;
- hypothesis must remain identifiable as hypothesis.

The categories organize observation.

They do not replace source criticism or evidentiary judgment.

---

## 8. Use in summaries, retrieval, and AI systems

When historical material is summarized, translated, indexed, retrieved, or processed by an AI system, the distinction among Fact, Perception, and Record should remain visible where it materially affects meaning.

A downstream system should not:

- convert a reported belief into an established fact;
- treat a later narrative as a transparent description of an earlier event;
- remove source distance;
- present a record's claim as though the record itself proved that claim;
- erase uncertainty while shortening a passage.

For example:

```text
Record:
A later chronicle states that the ruler received a divine sign.

Perception:
The narrative presents divine approval as part of the ruler's legitimacy.

Fact:
Whether the reported sign occurred cannot be established from the chronicle alone.
```

The same passage can therefore contain information relevant to more than one category.

The task is not to force every sentence into only one box.

The task is to keep the analytical distinctions visible.

---

## 9. What Source Trifurcation does not claim

Source Trifurcation does not claim that:

- every historical question can be solved by three categories alone;
- all records are equally useful;
- all perceptions have equal historical importance;
- all competing claims have equal evidentiary support;
- factual reconstruction is impossible;
- uncertainty prevents historical judgment;
- material evidence is automatically neutral;
- written records are inherently superior to other evidence;
- historical actors' beliefs should be accepted as factual merely because those beliefs influenced action.

It is a reading discipline, not a substitute for historical research.

Source criticism, archaeology, philology, chronology, area studies, material analysis, comparative research, and historiographical debate remain necessary.

---

## 10. Relationship to other methodology

Source Trifurcation is one part of the wider sanapedia historical-reading method.

It should be read together with:

- SANA Historical Reading Protocol — the broader reading and documentation standard;
- Three-Layer Analysis — structural analysis through Survival, Narrative, and Institution;
- Revision and Citation Policy — preservation of source conditions, epistemic status, and provenance during citation and transformation;
- Article Specification — separation of metadata responsibilities from substantive historical analysis.

These documents answer different questions.

Source Trifurcation asks:

> What kind of historical information are we looking at?

Three-Layer Analysis asks:

> Where are structural pressures, narratives, and institutions operating?

Revision and Citation Policy asks:

> What must remain traceable when the material changes or is reused?

Article Specification asks:

> How is the knowledge object represented and maintained?

Keeping these questions separate helps prevent a surviving narrative, an actor's belief, an analytical model, and a historical event from collapsing into a single undifferentiated claim.
