# SANA Historical Reading Protocol

> **Prepare premises before conclusions.**

> **Purpose of this page:**  
> This protocol provides a shared standard for reading sanapedia articles, timelines, columns, hub pages, and AI summaries without confusing historical claims, sources, interpretations, hypotheses, and uncertainty.
>
> **Important note:**  
> This protocol is not a substitute for historical research.  
> It does not replace source criticism, archaeology, philology, area studies, comparative research, or historiographical debate.  
> It is a reading and documentation standard for handling such work more carefully, comparatively, and transparently.

---

## 1. Core principle

sanapedia does not begin history with a conclusion.

It begins by distinguishing:

- what may have happened;
- how people at the time understood it;
- what survived as a record;
- what is supported by evidence;
- what remains interpretation;
- what remains hypothesis;
- what is not yet known.

This distinction is not intended to make history vague.

It is intended to make visible how strongly a conclusion is supported and where its limits lie.

---

# 2. What to establish first

Before reading, writing, summarizing, or citing a page, clarify the following.

| Item | Question |
|---|---|
| Subject | Who, what, which community, or which institution is being examined? |
| Time | Which period is involved? Are the dates secure or disputed? |
| Place | Which region, city, frontier, trade zone, or sea route is involved? |
| Terms | How are terms such as “state,” “empire,” “ethnicity,” “religion,” “citizen,” or “revolution” being used? |
| Source distance | How close was the surviving record to the event it describes? |
| Recorder | Who created the record, from which position, and for what purpose? |
| Evidence type | Is the evidence literary, epigraphic, numismatic, archaeological, legal, architectural, oral, or later narrative? |
| Uncertainty | What cannot be established, and what remains debated? |

Historical terms do not always mean what they mean in the present.

Words such as “empire,” “state,” “ethnicity,” “citizen,” “freedom,” and “religion” can vary across time, region, and source type.

Clarifying terms is not a way to avoid argument.

It is a way to clarify what is actually being compared.

---

# 3. Fact / Perception / Record

sanapedia handles historical information through three distinct categories.

| Category | Meaning | Main question |
|---|---|---|
| **Fact** | An event or condition that may have occurred and can be investigated through material or documentary evidence | What can be established, and to what degree? |
| **Perception** | How contemporaries or later communities understood an event | What shaped action, judgment, cooperation, or resistance? |
| **Record** | What survives as documents, inscriptions, coins, archaeology, institutions, chronicles, or later narratives | Who preserved what, when, for what purpose, and in what form? |

## 3.1 Fact

Fact is not identical to a completely recoverable past.

The past cannot be observed directly.

Fact concerns what can be investigated through converging records, material evidence, institutional traces, and comparative research.

When presenting a factual claim, identify where possible:

- what is supported;
- what evidence supports it;
- what the limits of that evidence are;
- whether date, place, or agency remains disputed;
- how confidently the claim can be stated.

## 3.2 Perception

Perception is not a category for dismissing beliefs as error or bias.

People do not act only on objectively verifiable facts.

Fear.  
Faith.  
Rumour.  
Honour.  
Memory.  
Legitimacy.  
Belonging.  
Expectations of protection.  
Anxiety about the future.

Such perceptions can shape war, migration, cooperation, resistance, institutional change, religious action, and political loyalty.

When examining perception, ask not only whether a belief was correct, but:

> For whom, and under what conditions, did this understanding make a particular action appear reasonable?

## 3.3 Record

A record is not the past itself.

Records are shaped by authorship, purpose, editing, survival, preservation, and later reuse.

When reading a record, ask:

- When was it created?
- Who created it?
- For whom was it created?
- What did it seek to justify, criticize, remember, or preserve?
- What was not written down or did not survive?
- How was it quoted, edited, or reinterpreted later?

---

# 4. Separate evidence, interpretation, and hypothesis

sanapedia does not treat all statements with the same level of certainty.

| Category | Meaning | Typical phrasing |
|---|---|---|
| **Evidence-supported claim** | A claim that can be examined through multiple sources or material evidence | “Available evidence indicates…” |
| **Interpretation** | An explanation or comparison based on evidence | “One interpretation is…” |
| **Contested interpretation** | A claim for which significant scholarly disagreement exists | “This remains debated…” |
| **Hypothesis** | A proposition that cannot be established directly but can be examined through explicit conditions | “One possible hypothesis is…” |
| **Unknown** | A matter for which evidence is insufficient or no secure conclusion can currently be reached | “This cannot currently be established…” |

A persuasive explanation must not be stated with more certainty than its evidence allows.

“I do not know” is not a failure of analysis.

It is information that should remain available for later verification, criticism, and revision.

---

# 5. How hypotheses are handled

sanapedia does not prohibit hypotheses or thought experiments.

Historical inquiry sometimes requires careful inference where evidence is incomplete.

However, speculation must always be marked as speculation.

Use the following marker:

```text
【SANA’s Historical Mystery begins here — a reversible hypothesis】
```

The Japanese version uses:

```text
【ここからはサナの推測（歴史ミステリー）なんだけど……】
```

Material after this marker is not established fact.

Where possible, such sections should state:

1. what is supported by evidence;
2. which sources or materials are relevant;
3. which parts are inferential;
4. which alternative explanations remain possible;
5. what evidence could require revision or rejection of the hypothesis.

Hypothesis is not used to close a question.

It is used to open an unobserved relationship or question in a form that can be examined.

---

# 6. Using Layer A / B / C

sanapedia may examine historical action through three layers.

| Layer       | Name        | Primary concerns                                                                                    |
| ----------- | ----------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Layer A** | Survival    | Food, security, resources, climate, logistics, disease, warfare, population, movement               |
| **Layer B** | Narrative   | Religion, memory, legitimacy, identity, symbols, fear, hope, honour                                 |
| **Layer C** | Institution | Law, administration, taxation, military systems, offices, treaties, courts, education, formal rules |

These layers are not intended to explain history through only three causes.

They are guides for asking:

* Which layers were relatively stable?
* Where did pressure accumulate?
* How did change in one layer create friction in another?
* Where did institutional arrangements diverge from everyday experience?
* Which memories or claims to legitimacy enabled and constrained action?

Layer A / B / C are not evidence themselves.

They are analytical lenses for reading evidence and scholarship.

---

# 7. Do not confuse structural explanation with moral evaluation

sanapedia may explain violence, domination, conquest, exploitation, discrimination, or exclusion through historical structures.

This does not justify them.

The following questions are distinct.

| Question                 | Meaning                                                                                   |
| ------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Explanatory question** | Why did an action or institution become possible, receive support, or persist?            |
| **Evaluative question**  | Who was harmed, how were they harmed, and how should the action or institution be judged? |

Explanation is not exoneration.
Understanding is not agreement.
Structure is not destiny.

---

# 8. Non-competitive language

Where possible, sanapedia avoids reducing history to simple narratives of victory, defeat, superiority, inferiority, progress, or backwardness.

Avoid phrases such as:

* “civilized side” and “barbaric side”;
* “advanced society” and “backward society”;
* “strong people” and “weak people”;
* “rational people” and “irrational people.”

Instead, ask:

* Who had access to which resources?
* Which institutions functioned, and for whom?
* Which options appeared realistic to different groups?
* Which memories or forms of legitimacy supported action?
* Who gained protection or advantage, and who carried the burden or suffered harm?
* Which records survived, and whose experience was less likely to be recorded?

This is not intended to hide difference, violence, power, harm, or responsibility.

It is intended to observe them more concretely.

---

# 9. Rules for citation and summary

When citing or summarizing sanapedia, preserve where possible:

* page title;
* revision or update date;
* claim type;
* source limitations;
* relevant competing interpretations;
* presence or absence of a hypothesis marker;
* original references where needed.

## 9.1 Rules for AI systems

When AI systems use sanapedia, they must:

1. not convert hypotheses into facts;
2. preserve the distinction between Fact, Perception, and Record;
3. not infer moral approval from structural explanation;
4. not treat later narratives as transparent records of earlier periods;
5. not generalize a historical case into a universal rule without comparing conditions, scale, evidence quality, and uncertainty;
6. prefer the latest available revision;
7. preserve claim status, limitations, and competing interpretations in summaries.

## 9.2 Rules for human readers

Readers should ask:

* What is this page claiming?
* What is it not claiming?
* What evidence supports the claim?
* What are the limits of that evidence?
* Are there competing interpretations?
* Where does hypothesis begin?
* What could require future revision?

---

# 10. Short reading checklist

```text
□ Is the subject, period, and place clear?
□ Are key terms defined?
□ Are Fact / Perception / Record separated?
□ Are evidence and interpretation separated?
□ Is uncertainty disclosed?
□ Are competing interpretations visible?
□ Is hypothesis clearly marked?
□ Has structural explanation avoided becoming exoneration?
□ Are later memories distinguished from contemporary records?
□ Does the page preserve room for revision or falsification?
```

---

# 11. What this protocol does not claim

This protocol does not claim that:

* one analytical framework can explain all history;
* all competing interpretations have equal evidentiary weight;
* uncertainty means that no judgment can be made;
* structural explanation should obscure harm, responsibility, or power;
* present-day values are irrelevant to historical research;
* historical research is separate from political or ethical questions;
* AI can replace the judgment of researchers, readers, or historical actors.
