# Timeline of the Fifteenth-Century Ottoman Empire

> **Research Status:** Provisional  
> **Coverage:** 1402–1481  
> **Central Question:** After fragmentation following the Battle of Ankara, how did the Ottoman dynasty reconnect Anatolia, Rumelia, the Balkans, the straits, the Black Sea, the Aegean, towns, military power, revenue, and religious communities—and how did it reshape these connections into a more imperial form of government during the late fifteenth century?  
> **Note:** This is not a timeline of conquest alone. It treats victory, defeat, treaty, urban acquisition, succession crisis, taxation, migration, architecture, law, and religious community as moments when the connections of government changed.

---

# Historical Framing

After Bayezid I was defeated by Timur at Ankara in 1402, Ottoman dynastic rule divided among several princes.

In 1413, Mehmed Çelebi defeated Musa Çelebi and began reunification as Mehmed I.

Murad II ruled from 1421 to 1444 and again from 1446 to 1451. Mehmed II ruled from 1444 to 1446 and again from 1451 to 1481.

On 29 May 1453, Mehmed II's forces took Constantinople. The city was then reorganized as a major imperial center of Ottoman rule.

During the later fifteenth century, military, diplomatic, urban, fiscal, and administrative change affected Serbia, Morea, Trebizond, Bosnia, the Black Sea, the Aegean, Anatolia, and the eastern Mediterranean.

Chronological events do not mean that complete rule began on one day and ended on another.

Towns, ports, villages, local elites, military actors, religious communities, and traders experienced change at different speeds and under different conditions.

---

# How to Read This Timeline

## 1. Empire Does Not Appear All at Once

The fifteenth-century Ottoman polity did not simply return to its pre-1402 form.

Through the Interregnum, reunification, Balkan war, the fall of Constantinople, urban rebuilding, and expansion around the Black Sea, Aegean, Mediterranean, Anatolia, and Balkans, the form of government itself changed.

Imperial transformation included:

- Expansion of territory
- Growth in the number and scale of towns under rule
- Larger military demands
- More complex management of land and revenue
- New relationships with religious communities
- Greater importance of straits, ports, roads, and rivers
- Reorganization of relationships among sultan, viziers, scribes, military actors, and regional elites

## 2. The Interregnum, 1402–1413, Was Not a Blank

The Interregnum was not a period without rulers.

It was a period in which:

- Süleyman Çelebi
- İsa Çelebi
- Musa Çelebi
- Mehmed Çelebi

attempted to govern from different towns, military networks, coinage, diplomatic relations, and regional communities.

The period reveals that even when dynastic authority fragmented, towns, military actors, revenue, ports, roads, and local elites still carried practical government.

## 3. Acquiring a Town Begins Government

Taking Bursa, Edirne, Thessaloniki, Serbian towns, Trebizond, or Constantinople did not mean only taking walls.

To govern a town required attention to:

- Population
- Water
- Food
- Ports
- Markets
- Artisans
- Religious institutions
- Revenue
- Courts
- Defense
- Roads
- Surrounding rural production

Urban acquisition was not the end of military action. It was the beginning of governing burden.

| Claimant | Main base | Main connections | Structural question |
|---|---|---|---|
| Süleyman Çelebi | Edirne and Rumelia | Towns, ports, trade, Byzantium, Venice, Balkan military actors | Could rule based on towns and diplomacy sustain authority? |

- Each claimant tried to govern through towns, markets, military actors, rural production, roads, ports, treaty, coinage, and religious institutions.
- It was a period of fragmentation, but also a period that exposed the practical connections supporting Ottoman government.

| Event | Peace agreement between Ottoman government and Venice |
| Main actors | Mehmed II, Venice, coastal towns, merchants, port communities, fleets |

### Timeline Notes

- Peace could allow the revival of commercial routes, port activity, customs, markets, and diplomatic exchange.
- But treaties did not distribute benefit evenly across all towns, traders, military actors, or rural communities.
- Maritime recovery could strengthen state and urban revenue while also creating new regional disparities.

### Reading Anatolia

Anatolia was not automatically stable because it was the dynasty's original core.

During the Interregnum, several princes and former principalities competed there. Later in the fifteenth century, relationships involving households, land, towns, religious institutions, military actors, and local legitimacy continued to require adjustment.

To govern Anatolia was not only to win campaigns.

It was also to ask:

> How can regional resources and memories be reconnected to central government?

# GMM: Observing Fifteenth-Century Ottoman Governing Capacity

| Layer | Demand | Supply | Main gap |
|---|---|---|---|
| A — Survival | Military force, ports, food, roads, horses, fleets, towns, defense, pay | Rural production, towns, revenue, land income, trade, ports, military actors, forts | Burden of wide warfare and urban reconstruction |
| B — Narrative | Dynastic legitimacy, meaning of conquest, reception by religious communities, imperial titles | Bloodline, coinage, buildings, victory, endowment, ritual, law | Same rule carried different meanings for different groups |
| C — Institution | Land, tax, military service, law, ports, towns, religious communities, succession, diplomacy | Viziers, scribes, military actors, local practice, towns, religious institutions | Difference between central reorganization speed and local adaptation |

## Central GMM Question

Fifteenth-century Ottoman government can be read as an attempt to expand Layers A, B, and C at the same time.

- Layer A required military capacity, towns, ports, food, coinage, roads, and fleets.
- Layer B required connection among dynasty, conquest, Islam, Roman legitimacy, and multi-confessional urban rule.
- Layer C required land, tax, service, courts, office, community, and town administration to operate across wider spaces.

These layers did not always move together.

Military expansion could burden towns and villages.

Reorganization of land, revenue, and endowment could increase state capacity while generating friction with households and communities.

A new imperial narrative could spread without erasing earlier regional memory and rights.

# 【SANA’s Historical Mystery begins here — a reversible hypothesis】

The fifteenth-century Ottoman Empire may have been less a state that “simply grew quickly” than:

> A polity that repeatedly repaired broken connections while enlarging the scale of connection itself.

In 1402, the dynastic center broke.

In 1413, Mehmed I attempted to make connections among towns, military actors, revenue, and diplomacy function again.

Under Murad II, succession, warfare, viziers, Janissaries, regional elites, and Balkan powers had to be kept in workable relation.

After 1453, Mehmed II may have done more than expand territory.

He may have tried to reconnect:

- Straits
- A new capital
- Ports
- Religious communities
- Officials
- Military actors
- Land
- Revenue
- Law
- Knowledge
- Diplomacy
- Households and regional society

to a stronger center.

But stronger connection did not mean safety or prosperity for everyone.

A stronger center could rebuild towns and organize defense, while also making military service, taxation, land redistribution, migration, religious pressure, political purging, and intervention in local rights more direct.

Imperial transformation may therefore have been:

> A design increasing the state's capacity to survive,

and also:

> A design redistributing the burdens of that state among regions, communities, and people.

# What This Timeline Does Not Claim

- It does not claim that Ottoman government before 1402 was fully unified or stable.
- It does not treat the Interregnum as an empty period without government.
- It does not treat Mehmed I's reunification as a complete restoration of Bayezid I's earlier order.
- It does not reduce Murad II's abdication and restoration to one personal motive.
- It does not reduce the Buçuktepe rebellion to one cause.
- It does not explain the fall of Constantinople by military capacity alone.
- It treats post-1453 resettlement as a process involving reconstruction, burden, coercion, and renegotiation.
- It does not treat annexation as immediate and uniform social or administrative integration.
- It does not treat Otlukbeli as a simple Ottoman-versus-East conflict.
- It does not treat maritime power as automatic proof of complete control.
- It does not describe centralization as simple progress or decline.
- It does not treat structural explanation as justification for war, conquest, plunder, taxation, execution, forced migration, enslavement, religious pressure, or inequality.
- It does not treat Sana's speculation as verified historical fact.
