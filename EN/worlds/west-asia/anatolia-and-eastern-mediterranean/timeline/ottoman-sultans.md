# Timeline of Ottoman Rulers

> **Research Status:** Provisional  
> **Coverage:** Approximately 1299–1922  
> **Central Question:** How did Ottoman dynastic rule move from frontier-based political connections to an imperial form of government spanning multiple regions, religious communities, and military institutions—and where did it fragment, reconnect, and reorganize along the way?  
> **Note:** This is not a list of “strong rulers.” It records accession, abdication, interregnum, shared or uncertain authority, palace crisis, regional autonomy, and succession conflict as moments when the connections of government changed.

---

# 【確実なこと（歴史的事実）】 / Historical Framing

The earliest Ottoman rulers cannot be described with the same chronological certainty as later rulers.

In particular, historians debate:

- When an Ottoman state can be said to have begun
- Whether 1299 should be fixed as Osman's exact accession date
- Whether Orhan's accession should be dated to 1324, 1326, or understood as a transition
- Whether early rulers should be called “sultans” in the later imperial sense
- Whether 1402–1413 can be described as the reign of any single ruler

This timeline therefore treats early dates as widely used chronological reference points, not as perfectly fixed moments of dynastic origin.

---

# How to Read This Timeline

## 1. A Reign Does Not Mean Complete Control

A ruler's reign indicates the period in which that person was the principal dynastic ruler.

It does not mean that every region obeyed with the same intensity.

At different times:

- Regional powerholders retained strong autonomy
- Brothers, uncles, and princes competed
- Outside powers supported rival claimants
- Towns offered conditional loyalty
- Some regions remained under competing dynasties, principalities, or empires

A reign date is therefore not the same thing as uniform control over every territory.

## 2. The Interregnum, 1402–1413, Is Treated Separately

After Bayezid I's defeat at Ankara, Ottoman rule divided among several princes.

During this period:

- Süleyman Çelebi
- İsa Çelebi
- Musa Çelebi
- Mehmed Çelebi

used different regions, towns, military actors, and diplomatic ties as bases of authority.

The Interregnum is not treated as an empty gap before Mehmed I.

It is treated as:

> A period in which Ottoman government divided into several regional survival strategies.

## 3. Succession Crisis Is Not Only Personal Ambition

Succession conflict did not emerge only from the personalities of princes.

It also involved:

- Military pay and status
- Urban security
- Local land and tax revenue
- Roles of viziers and bureaucratic households
- Relations with outside powers
- Positions of religious communities
- Questions of who could represent dynastic legitimacy

# III. Ottoman Interregnum — Fragmented Centers of Rule

## 1402–1413: Ottoman Interregnum

> **Central Question:** When the dynastic center collapses, where do towns, military power, revenue, land, diplomacy, and legitimacy reconnect?

After Ankara, Ottoman rule did not continue under one uncontested ruler.

This period should not be treated as an empty space or a simple interruption.

Different princes tried to govern through different regional bases and resource networks.

| Claimant | Main period | Main base or region | Structural emphasis |
|---|---:|---|---|
| Süleyman Çelebi | 1402–1411 | Rumelia, Edirne | Towns, trade, diplomacy, Balkan military actors |
| İsa Çelebi | 1402–1403 | Bursa, Karesi, western Anatolia | Bursa, seniority, western Anatolia, diplomacy |
| Mehmed Çelebi | 1402–1413 | Amasya, inland Anatolia | Regional integration and durable military-administrative capacity |
| Musa Çelebi | 1411–1413 | Rumelia, Edirne | Military power, coinage, offices, reorganization of Rumelian rule |

| Main centers | Amasya, Bursa, Edirne, Rumelia |
| Structural significance | Reconnection of land, revenue, military actors, towns, and diplomacy after civil war |

### Timeline Notes

- After Musa's defeat in 1413, Mehmed became the sole major ruler of the Ottoman dynasty.

- Naval conflict with Venice, movements associated with Sheikh Bedreddin, and Balkan-Anatolian adjustment show that instability remained after unification.

- Abdication and restoration showed both dynastic flexibility and tension over who possessed final decision-making authority.

# V. Imperial Transformation

## 1444–1446, 1451–1481: Mehmed II

| Field | Content |
|---|---|
| Name | Mehmed II |
| Japanese name | メフメト2世 |
| First reign | 1444–1446 |
| Second reign | 1451–1481 |
| Main centers | Edirne, Manisa, Bursa, Istanbul |
| Structural significance | Imperial reorganization of capital, military power, bureaucracy, law, land, religious communities, and Bosporus control |
| Major challenge | Transforming post-conquest territory and city into durable government |

### Timeline Notes

- During his first reign, authority was unstable among the young Mehmed, Murad II, viziers, Janissaries, and outside powers.
- After the Buçuktepe rebellion in 1446, Mehmed moved to Manisa and Murad II returned.
- After his renewed accession in 1451, Mehmed built Rumeli Hisarı and took Constantinople in 1453.
- After 1453, Istanbul was reorganized not only as a memorial of military victory but as an imperial center of population, ports, religious communities, law, endowment, trade, education, and bureaucracy.
- The incorporation of Trebizond in 1461, war with Venice from 1463 to 1479, and victory at Otlukbeli in 1473 expanded the geographical scale and resource burden of imperial rule.
- After his death in 1481, conflict emerged between Bayezid II and Cem Sultan.

# SANA Structural Reading

## Long-Term Dynastic Questions

This timeline suggests that the Ottoman dynasty did not confront the same problem in every period.

| Period | Main Structural Question |
|---|---|
| Osman I / Orhan | How can military, town, and community connections be formed locally? |
| Murad I / Bayezid I | How can wider rule and rapid integration be sustained? |
| Interregnum | When the center disappears, where does government remain? |
| Mehmed I / Murad II | How can fragmented rule become repeatable government again? |
| Mehmed II | How can city, military, bureaucracy, and religious communities be connected at imperial scale? |
| Sixteenth century | How can resources, law, military power, and provincial government sustain a large empire? |
| Seventeenth–eighteenth centuries | How can government adapt to changes in warfare, coinage, provinces, court, and trade? |
| Nineteenth century | How can reform, central control, and international pressure be handled? |
| Early twentieth century | How can imperial dynastic rule coexist with or survive the rise of nation-state sovereignty? |

## The Interregnum as an Analytical Window

The Ottoman Interregnum of 1402–1413 is often treated as exceptional disorder.

But it exposes structures of government that are normally harder to see.

- Which claimant did towns admit?
- Whose orders did military actors follow?
- Whose rule did land and taxation support?
- Whom did outside powers choose as a negotiating partner?
- How far could bloodline function without resources and security?

In this sense, the Interregnum was not only “broken time.”

It was also:

> A period that made visible where the connections supporting Ottoman government actually existed.

# What This Timeline Does Not Claim

- It does not treat 1299 as an absolutely certain founding date of the Ottoman state.
- It does not claim that early reign dates, titles, or territorial scope are fully settled.
- It does not claim that a reign date means complete and uniform control of every region.
- It does not treat the Interregnum as an empty period without government.
- It does not explain succession conflict only through personality or ambition.
- It does not narrate Ottoman history as a single line of rise, stagnation, and decline.
- It does not treat centralization as simple progress or decline.
- It does not treat reform as a one-way process in which the center acts and society merely receives.
- It does not romanticize imperial government by separating it from warfare, taxation, forced migration, execution, religious pressure, and domination.
- It does not treat SANA structural explanation as an excuse for historical violence or inequality.
