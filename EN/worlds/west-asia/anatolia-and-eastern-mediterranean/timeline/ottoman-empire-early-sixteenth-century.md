# Ottoman Empire in the Early Sixteenth Century
## 1512–1566: Succession, East, Former Mamluk Lands, Hungary, Sea, Law, and Princes

> **Research Status:** Provisional  
> **Central Question:** During the reigns of Selim I and Süleyman I, how did Ottoman government attempt to keep expanding military, fiscal, administrative, maritime, religious-legitimating, and dynastic burdens connected?  
>
> **Note:** This is not a conquest list. It treats campaigns, succession, provincial rebellion, law, survey, ports, towns, waqf, princely households, and diplomacy as connected dimensions of government.

---

# Human Overview

From 1512 to 1566, Ottoman government expanded or deepened its involvement across Anatolia, Syria, Egypt, the Hejaz, Iraq, Hungary, North Africa, the Mediterranean, and the Red Sea.

This period should not be reduced to an “age of expansion.”

It also involved:

- Dynastic competition continuing from the end of Bayezid II’s reign
- The Shahkulu rebellion and instability in eastern Anatolia
- Military, political, and local tensions with the Safavid polity
- Administrative connection of Syria, Egypt, the Hejaz, Red Sea, and former Mamluk territories
- Long-term military and diplomatic pressure around Hungary and the Habsburg frontier
- Maritime force requiring ports, ships, timber, sailors, artisans, and coastal supply
- Continuing adjustment of timar, tax, waqf, survey, urban markets, and military pay
- Succession anxiety around Süleyman’s sons
- Reallocation of roles among grand viziers, palace, governors, military actors, and provincial society

This timeline is a shared spine for person and event pages.

# Source Problem

## The Phrase “Golden Age”

Süleyman’s reign is often described as a golden age.

That language can help identify the importance of:

- Military power
- Maritime capacity
- Architecture
- Law
- Literature
- Bureaucratic practice

But it can also hide:

- Requisition and supply for campaigns
- Military pay
- Tax and movement within provincial society
- Friction in former Mamluk territories
- Safavid-frontier pressure
- Execution of princes and dismissal of grand viziers
- Demand for ports, ships, forests, and skilled labor
- Food, prices, housing, and disaster in towns
- Adjustment of waqf revenue and older rights

This timeline does not decide whether the period was golden or declining.

It provides a basis for asking:

> Who connected the enlarged range of government, through what resources, and with what limits?

# Fact / Perception / Record

## Fact

- Selim I ruled from 1512 to 1520.
- Süleyman I ruled from 1520 to 1566.
- Safavid relations, former Mamluk territories, Hungary, the Habsburg frontier, Mediterranean affairs, and Red Sea–Indian Ocean questions became major parts of Ottoman government.
- Chaldiran, the incorporation of Egypt, Belgrade, Rhodes, Mohács, Preveza, Amasya, and Szigetvár are major chronological thresholds.
- The executions of Princes Mustafa and Bayezid are essential for understanding succession anxiety under Süleyman.

## Perception

- The central court may have viewed military, revenue, provincial administration, ports, and succession as parts of one imperial order.
- Local communities may have experienced the same campaigns or institutional changes as protection, burden, opportunity, taxation, movement, military service, or danger.
- Military actors may have prioritized pay, promotion, booty, honor, supply, and trust in command.
- Habsburg, Venetian, Safavid, Portuguese, and former Mamluk actors may have understood Ottoman expansion through different security, commercial, religious, and dynastic concerns.

## Record

- Ottoman chronicles, Süleymannâmes, law codes, survey registers, waqf deeds, architecture, coinage, envoy reports, and Safavid, Mamluk, Venetian, Habsburg, French, and Portuguese materials survive.
- Each record has military, administrative, religious, commercial, dynastic, or diplomatic purposes.
- A record may show that something happened, but does not automatically reveal the experience of all subjects or the interior motives of one person.

## Sana’s Structural Hypothesis — Not Established Historical Fact

The central problem from 1512 to 1566 may not have been only:

> “How far can Ottoman power expand?”

It may also have been:

- How can a stable frontier be made where the Safavids cannot be entirely removed?
- How can Syria, Egypt, the Hejaz, and the Red Sea be connected with older elites, towns, pilgrimage, and revenue?
- How can Hungary be governed as a question of forts, tributaries, provinces, tax, and garrisons rather than battle alone?
- How can ships, ports, timber, sailors, artisans, food, and customs be sustained across the Mediterranean, Red Sea, and Indian Ocean?
- How can law, survey, waqf, and urban complexes make expanded rule more predictable?
- How can succession anxiety be contained before princes combine with military actors and regional households?

From this perspective, the reign of Süleyman I appears not as a finished system but as:

> A period in which an enlarged empire repeatedly tried to absorb the burdens generated by its own expansion through law, revenue, towns, military organization, diplomacy, and dynastic punishment.

This is a structural hypothesis, not established historical fact.
