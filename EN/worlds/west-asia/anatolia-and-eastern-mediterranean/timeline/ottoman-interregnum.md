# Timeline of the Ottoman Interregnum

> **Central Question:** After the Battle of Ankara in 1402, when the Ottoman dynastic center broke apart, to whom did towns, military actors, revenue, diplomacy, and dynastic legitimacy reconnect?

## Historical Framing

The Ottoman Interregnum was the period after Bayezid I's defeat at Ankara in 1402, when several of his sons competed for rule.

It should not be treated as an empty gap.

Different princes attempted to govern through different regional bases, towns, military networks, coins, offices, treaties, and diplomatic relationships.

In 1413, Mehmed Çelebi defeated Musa Çelebi and began reunification as Mehmed I.

---

# How to Read This Timeline

## 1. The Interregnum Was Not the Absence of Government

The question is not only which prince was legitimate.

It is also:

- Who could protect towns?
- Who could pay soldiers?
- Who could control ports and crossings?
- Who could negotiate with Byzantium, Venice, Serbia, and Wallachia?
- Who could use the symbolic authority of Bursa or Edirne?

## 2. Each Prince Had a Different Survival Strategy

| Prince | Main base | Structural profile |
|---|---|---|
| Süleyman Çelebi | Edirne, Rumelia, Gallipoli | Rumelian government through towns, ports, diplomacy, and trade |
| İsa Çelebi | Bursa, western Anatolia | Dynastic seniority, former capital, and regional principalities |
| Musa Çelebi | Rumelia, Edirne, Danube zone | Reorganization through military actors, coinage, and office |
| Mehmed Çelebi | Amasya, Tokat, Bursa | Inland base, regional military actors, gradual reunification |

# Timeline

## 1402 — Battle of Ankara

Bayezid I was defeated by Timur.

The defeat did not only remove one ruler from the battlefield.

It destabilized:

- the Anatolia–Rumelia connection,
- relationships with former Anatolian principalities,
- the political position of Bursa and Edirne,
- pay and land relations for military actors,
- diplomatic authority,
- and the perception of dynastic legitimacy.

## 1402–1403 — Süleyman Çelebi Secures Rumelia

Süleyman moved from Anatolia toward Gallipoli and established himself in Rumelia, with Edirne as his principal center.

His strength was not military force alone.

He could connect:

- Edirne,
- Gallipoli,
- Byzantine diplomacy,
- Venetian and Genoese commercial relations,
- Balkan towns and military actors.

## c. 1402–1406 — İsa Çelebi and Bursa

İsa struggled for Bursa and western Anatolia.

Bursa was not merely a town.

It carried early Ottoman dynastic memory, tombs, markets, revenue, religious institutions, and symbolic capital.

İsa's strategy can be read as an attempt to claim the dynastic center through Bursa.

## 1403–1404 — Mehmed Çelebi Enters Bursa

Mehmed used Amasya and Tokat as an inland base and pushed into Bursa after defeating İsa.

His strength was not sudden control of all major towns.

It was the ability to connect regional military actors, local elites, towns, revenue, and rural resources over time.

## 1409 — Musa Çelebi Crosses into Rumelia

Mehmed sent Musa toward Rumelia in order to draw Süleyman back from Anatolia.

This was not simply the movement of one prince.

It required:

- soldiers,
- vessels,
- Danube crossings,
- Wallachian involvement,
- Serbian connections,
- Rumelian military actors,
- roads and supply.

## 1410 — Fighting Near Kosmidion

Musa fought Süleyman and was initially defeated.

This episode shows the complexity of alliance.

Byzantium, Serbia, Wallachia, and Rumelian military actors did not act according to a simple religious or ethnic binary.

They made choices shaped by security, land, tribute, households, towns, and military prospects.

## 1411 — Musa Çelebi Takes Edirne

Musa took Edirne, issued coinage, appointed officials, and declared rule.

This matters because it shows that the Interregnum was not a time without state functions.

It was a time when several centers operated fragments of government.

Musa's government is also associated with the appointment of Sheikh Bedreddin as kazasker.

## 1411–1413 — Musa and Pressure on Byzantium

Musa applied pressure to Constantinople and was active around the Black Sea coast and Thessaly.

Byzantium was both an enemy and a diplomatic actor capable of supporting rival Ottoman claimants.

Musa's pressure against Byzantium was therefore not only about taking a city.

It was also about limiting Byzantine intervention in dynastic competition.

## 1412 — Mehmed Crosses into Rumelia

Mehmed crossed into Rumelia with Byzantine assistance and fought Musa, but was initially defeated.

This reveals that Mehmed did not win through isolated military capacity.

He had to reorganize connections among Byzantium, Rumelian military actors, Serbian forces, akıncı groups, and Anatolian resources.

## 1413 — Battle of Çamurlu

Mehmed defeated Musa in 1413 and declared himself the sole ruler in Edirne.

But reunification was not the end of the problem.

It opened new tasks:

- how to treat Musa's supporters,
- how to reconnect Rumelian military actors,
- how to integrate Bursa and Edirne,
- how to reorganize revenue and land,
- how to renegotiate with Byzantium and Balkan powers,
- and how to rebuild trust after civil war.

# Source Trifurcation

## Fact

- Ottoman dynastic rule fragmented after Ankara in 1402.
- Süleyman operated from Rumelia and Edirne.
- İsa contested Bursa and western Anatolia.
- Mehmed used Amasya as a major base and later held Bursa.
- Musa crossed into Rumelia and took Edirne in 1411.
- In 1413 Mehmed defeated Musa and began reunification.

# 【SANA’s Historical Mystery begins here — a reversible hypothesis】

The deepest meaning of the Ottoman Interregnum may not be “who won.”

It may be that:

> When the dynastic center disappeared, the practical connections supporting government became visible.

Süleyman used Rumelian towns and diplomacy.

İsa used Bursa and dynastic memory.

Musa tried to rebuild government in Rumelia through military actors, coinage, and office.

Mehmed connected inland resources and towns gradually, then reunified the dynasty.

The Interregnum was not only failure.

It was also a moment that exposed the life-support systems of government: towns, soldiers, revenue, memory, and diplomacy.

# What This Timeline Does Not Claim

- It does not treat the Interregnum as total anarchy.
- It does not treat Mehmed I's victory as simple proof of superior legitimacy.
- It does not reduce Süleyman, İsa, or Musa to “losers.”
- It does not treat Byzantium or Balkan powers as outside spectators.
- It does not explain the princes' actions only through loyalty or betrayal.
- It does not treat structural explanation as justification for war, execution, plunder, coercion, or domination.
- It does not treat Sana's speculation as verified historical fact.
