# Ottoman–Balkan Timeline in the Early Fifteenth Century

> **Research Status:** Provisional  
> **Coverage:** 1402–1453  
> **Regions:** Rumelia, Thrace, Edirne, Constantinople, Gallipoli, Serbia, Wallachia, Bulgaria, Macedonia, Thessaloniki, the Morea, Albania, Hungary, and the Danube frontier  
> **Central Question:** After the dynastic center of the Ottoman house fragmented following Ankara in 1402, how did towns, ports, military actors, rulers, rural communities, religious institutions, and diplomatic networks across Rumelia and the Balkans connect to different political centers? After reunification in 1413, how did Ottoman rule attempt to endure through combinations of war, treaty, tribute, urban government, and regional negotiation?  
> **Note:** This is not a timeline of Ottoman conquest alone. It follows war, treaty, migration, tribute, urban defense, local powerholders, military actors, religious communities, ports, and trade as moments when the connections of government changed.

---

# Historical Framing

After the Battle of Ankara in 1402, Ottoman rule did not remain united under one uncontested ruler.

Several princes made claims through different regional bases.

In Rumelia, Süleyman Çelebi operated from Edirne from 1402 to 1411, and Musa Çelebi from 1411 to 1413.

Mehmed Çelebi maintained an Anatolian base around Amasya and defeated Musa in 1413, becoming Mehmed I and beginning reunification.

Under Murad II, from 1421 onward, rival claimants, Byzantium, Venice, Serbia, Wallachia, Hungary, and frontier military actors became involved in overlapping wars, treaties, truces, and political realignment.

Chronological entries do not mean that control began completely on one day and ended completely on another.

Towns, ports, villages, regional rulers, military actors, and religious communities experienced changes in power at different speeds and under different conditions.

# How to Read This Timeline

## 1. Rumelia Was Not Simply a Conquered Space

Rumelia and the Balkans contained:

- Towns such as Edirne, Thessaloniki, Gallipoli, Serres, Sofia, Skopje, Novo Brdo, Belgrade, Smederevo, and Constantinople
- Routes across the Danube, Bosporus, Aegean, Black Sea, roads, and inland corridors
- Political formations in Serbia, Wallachia, Bulgaria, the Morea, Albania, and Hungary
- Frontier military actors, akıncı, garrisons, local elites, mercenaries, merchants, sailors, and rural communities
- Orthodox, Catholic, Muslim, Jewish, and other religious or communal networks

Government did not simply erase these connections.

It also reorganized them through warfare, tribute, treaty, marriage, migration, markets, military service, and law.

## 2. Avoid “Ottomans versus Balkans” as a Fixed Binary

Political relationships were not simple ethnic, religious, or civilizational oppositions.

For example:

- Balkan rulers could support an Ottoman prince
- Ottoman princes could seek Byzantine assistance
- Venice could make agreements with Ottoman rulers
- Serbian and Wallachian military actors could choose among multiple political centers
- Ottoman military figures could make decisions shaped by regional society, trade, household, and local ties

This timeline treats relationships not as fixed categories of friend and enemy, but as connections shaped by resources, security, revenue, towns, land, dynastic succession, and diplomacy.

## 3. Battles Are Reorganization Points, Not Endpoints

A battle changes more than a territorial line.

After battle, questions emerge:

- Who controls city gates?
- Who pays soldiers?
- Who collects rural revenue?
- Who maintains ports, roads, and fortresses?
- Who supports markets, religious institutions, and endowments?
- Who negotiates with outside powers?

A battle therefore creates new governing problems as well as military outcomes.

| Regional impact | Ottoman dynastic authority fragmented; Anatolia and Rumelia became less securely connected |
| Structural significance | Dynastic descent alone could not hold together towns, military power, revenue, and diplomacy |

### Timeline Notes

- Bayezid's defeat was not only a military loss.
- Former principalities in Anatolia resumed political activity, while towns, military actors, and regional elites in Rumelia had to assess new dynastic centers.
- The question after 1402 was not only “Who is sultan?”
- It was also: who could defend towns, pay soldiers, collect revenue, uphold treaties, and maintain roads, ports, and fortresses?

- Diplomatic restraint can therefore be read not simply as weakness, but as a governing choice aimed at preserving urban and military resources.

- This shows that the Interregnum was not a period without government, but a period in which different forms of government competed.

- Abdication therefore became a test of continuity across finance, diplomacy, administration, military affairs, and urban order.

| Structural significance | Shows that military actors could intervene in the conditions of government through pay, livelihood, succession, and political authority |

### Timeline Notes

- The Buçuktepe rebellion cannot be explained through only one cause such as coinage, pay, vizierial action, or mistrust of a young ruler.
- War burden, military status, court competition, urban prices, succession, and external threat may all have overlapped.
- Janissaries cannot be treated merely as troops receiving orders.
- They were also actors bargaining over pay, rank, succession, and the center of government.

# VI. From Second Kosovo to 1453 — 1446 to 1453

| Field | Content |
|---|---|
| Date | 1446 |
| Event | Murad II returns as ruler |
| Main actors | Murad II, Mehmed II, Janissaries, viziers, regional military actors, external powers |

### Timeline Notes

- Murad's return can be interpreted as an attempt to restore military, diplomatic, and administrative continuity.
- At the same time, Mehmed's early experience of uncertain rule may matter for later political choices.
- Restoration did not eliminate the question of who designed policy and who translated command into military, fiscal, urban, and diplomatic practice.

- In the Morea, Albania, Serbia, and Wallachia, mountains, fortresses, ports, roads, ruling households, religious communities, and villages changed the conditions of government.
- Central military victory could not stabilize taxation, roads, markets, and military service without regional executors and integrators.
- Figures such as Turahan Bey were not merely executors of central orders. They were also regional integrators connected to settlement, endowment, towns, roads, and border defense.

# Regional Cross-Sections

## A. Edirne — A Node of Rumelian Government

| Function | What Edirne Connected |
|---|---|
| Dynastic authority | Princes, rulers, court, accession, succession |
| Military | Garrisons, frontier military actors, Balkan campaigns, roads |
| Administration | Scribes, coinage, courts, offices, orders |
| Urban life | Markets, artisans, religious institutions, rural supply |
| Diplomacy | Information and envoys involving Byzantium, Serbia, Wallachia, Hungary, and Venice |
| Memory | Political memory of the Interregnum, Musa, Murad II, and the prehistory of Mehmed II |

### Reading Edirne

Edirne was not merely a sign that Ottoman rule had reached Rumelia.

More importantly, it became:

> A political mechanism able to connect dynasty, military power, towns, coinage, roads, religion, diplomacy, and information at the same time.

---

## B. Gallipoli and the Straits — A Strong but Vulnerable Connection

| Function | Why It Mattered |
|---|---|
| Military crossing | Movement of forces between Anatolia and Rumelia |
| Diplomacy | Negotiation with Byzantium, Genoa, Venice, and claimants |
| Trade | Ships, ports, customs, goods, merchants |
| Intelligence | Envoys, information, exiles, and movement of princes |
| Security | Control of straits, port defense, prevention of outside aid |

### Reading Gallipoli and the Straits

Gallipoli and the straits were not simply the doorway through which Ottoman forces entered Europe.

During the Interregnum, whether princes crossed into Rumelia or returned to Anatolia, whether Byzantine or Genoese actors supported them, and who controlled vessels and ports could alter the balance of the entire dynasty.

The straits were therefore both a powerful connection and a vulnerable connection capable of producing renewed fragmentation.

## C. Danube, Serbia, and Wallachia — A Zone of Negotiation Rather Than a Fixed Border

| Element | Structural Role |
|---|---|
| Danube | Crossing, movement of goods, military boundary, trade, information |
| Fortresses | Urban defense, revenue, garrisons, refuge, regional government |
| Mines and towns | Silver, revenue, artisans, trade, military expenditure |
| Serbian rulers | Marriage, military service, tribute, adjustment between Hungary and the Ottomans |
| Wallachian rulers | Danube defense, crossings, military support, tribute, frontier government |
| Hungary | Large-scale northern military pressure, diplomacy, crusading, siege warfare |
| Local elites | Followers, land, villages, towns, links to multiple centers |

### Reading the Danube Zone

The Danube region cannot be seen as a simple line separating the Ottoman Empire and the Kingdom of Hungary.

It included rulers, garrisons, merchants, miners, villages, religious institutions, households, exiles, and envoys connected to more than one political center.

Control depended not only on who raised a flag, but on who could actually operate revenue, roads, military service, justice, gates, and trade.

|---|---|

### Reading the Aegean and Morea

Government in the Morea and Aegean could not be sustained by land armies alone.

Ports, vessels, coastal towns, mountains, roads, fortresses, trade, religious communities, and Venetian-Genoese information networks all mattered.

When siege or conquest occurred, political change also affected maritime movement and urban life.

# GMM: Observing Governing Capacity in Rumelia and the Balkans

| Layer | Demand | Supply | Main Gap |
|---|---|---|---|
| A — Survival | Warfare, food, roads, rivers, ports, fortresses, urban defense, soldiers' pay | Rural production, towns, taxation, trade, ships, regional military actors, rulers | Burdens of multiple fronts and long conflict |
| B — Narrative | Dynastic legitimacy, religious protection, urban safety, explanation of regional rule | Bloodline, coinage, treaty, buildings, victory, religious institutions, ritual | The same rule held different meanings for different groups |
| C — Institution | Tax, military service, courts, fortresses, ports, treaty, office, succession | Viziers, scribes, military actors, local custom, towns, religious institutions | Central command could become disconnected from regional roles |

## Central GMM Question

In early fifteenth-century Rumelia and the Balkans, Layers A, B, and C had to be sustained together.

- Military victory alone could not maintain ports, towns, roads, food, revenue, and soldiers' pay.
- Dynastic descent alone could not guarantee support from townspeople or frontier military actors.
- Treaties and offices alone could not guarantee practical cooperation in local society.

Government was:

> The work of repeatedly connecting military power, resources, narrative, and institutions at different speeds in different regions.

# 【SANA’s Historical Mystery begins here — a reversible hypothesis】

The early fifteenth-century Balkans may not have been only a stage on which the Ottoman dynasty “conquered Europe.”

They may also have been:

> A vast connective space in which towns, ports, roads, rivers, military actors, religious communities, and regional rulers repeatedly tested how life could continue when dynastic authority broke down.

During the Interregnum, princes possessed dynastic blood, but bloodline alone did not create government.

Holding Edirne, crossing the Danube, keeping markets open, paying soldiers, operating ports, making treaties, and securing cooperation from frontier military actors—these conditions made a dynastic claim look like rule.

Varna, Buçuktepe, and Second Kosovo can be read in the same way.

They were not only battles or rebellions.

They may have been moments when the region renegotiated:

> Who decides, who executes, who supplies resources, and who bears the burden of government?

---

# What This Timeline Does Not Claim

- It does not treat Rumelia and the Balkans as a simple binary of Ottomans versus non-Ottomans.
- It does not treat Serbia, Wallachia, Hungary, Byzantium, or Venice as uniform political blocs.
- It does not reduce the actions of claimants, military actors, and regional rulers to simple loyalty or betrayal.
- It does not treat treaty as proof of complete peace or uniform implementation.
- It does not treat capture of a town as immediate, uniform integration of residents, ports, markets, religious communities, and rural areas.
- It does not explain Kosovo, Varna, or Constantinople only through later national or religious memory.
- It does not treat Ottoman government as military conquest alone.
- It does not treat structural explanation as justification for war, plunder, punishment, forced migration, religious pressure, domination, enslavement, or inequality.
- It does not treat Sana's speculation as verified historical fact.
