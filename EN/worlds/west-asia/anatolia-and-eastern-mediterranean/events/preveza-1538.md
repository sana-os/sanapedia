# Preveza, 1538

> **Research Status:** Provisional  
> **Central Question:** Why did the Battle of Preveza become more than a one-day clash of fleets, reorganizing ports, islands, galleys, oarsmen, artillery, supply, Venice, Habsburg power, papal diplomacy, and Ottoman maritime government across the Aegean, Ionian, and Adriatic seas?  
>
> **Note:** This page does not treat Preveza as a simple miracle in which a smaller Ottoman fleet defeated a larger armada. It distinguishes ship type, shallow water, wind, port access, oarsmen, artillery, intelligence, allied command, and postwar island-port administration.

---

# Human Overview

On 28 September 1538, an Ottoman fleet under Barbaros Hayreddin Pasha fought a Holy League fleet commanded by Andrea Doria near Preveza.[^tdv-preveza]

The Holy League had been organized in 1538 through the mediation of Pope Paul III and brought together Venice, Habsburg and Spanish power, papal forces, Genoa, the Knights of Malta, Portugal, and related forces.[^tdv-preveza]

The immediate background included the Ottoman campaign toward Apulia and Corfu in 1537, Barbaros’s activity in the Cyclades and Sporades, and struggle around Venetian islands and maritime routes.[^tdv-preveza]

For Ottoman government, the issue was not merely whether enemy ships could be sunk.

It involved who could connect:

- Shipping between Istanbul and Egypt
- Aegean islands
- Ionian ports
- Adriatic coasts
- Sailors, oarsmen, and supply from Anatolia and Greece
- Venetian maritime networks
- Habsburg and Spanish naval power
- Ottoman-connected naval bases in North Africa

into durable circuits of security, taxation, commerce, and military movement.[^tdv-preveza][^guilmartin]

In June 1538, Barbaros left Istanbul with forty galleys.

He was later joined by additional vessels prepared at Istanbul, ships arriving from Egypt, and personnel gathered from Anatolian districts and islands.[^tdv-preveza]

The Ottoman fleet moved among islands and coasts while handling men, oarsmen, guns, food, intelligence, prize goods, and local revenue.

This was not a straight voyage toward one battle. It was a campaign in which islands, ports, requisition, supply, pressure, and commerce overlapped.[^tdv-preveza][^ertas-spoils]

The Holy League assembled a larger fleet in numerical terms, though exact figures vary sharply among sources.

What matters is that it brought together states with different interests, ship types, command systems, and fiscal bases: Venice, papal forces, Habsburg-Spanish power, Genoa, Malta, and others.[^tdv-preveza]

Preveza was shaped by shallow approaches around the Gulf of Narda, forts, anchorages, wind, and the different movement capacities of galleys and large sailing ships.

Large sailing vessels could carry powerful artillery, but could face limits in shallow water, narrow approaches, and weak winds. Oared galleys could move without wind and operate closer to coast and shallow water.[^tdv-preveza][^guilmartin]

By late September, Barbaros had repaired and strengthened the Preveza fortress and used the relationship between the gulf and open water in confronting the allied fleet.

Ottoman sources describe a crescent formation, artillery fire, and galley maneuver in the battle of 28 September.[^tdv-preveza]

The allied fleet attempted to envelop or divide the Ottoman formation but did not succeed.

Andrea Doria is recorded as ordering retreat toward Corfu.[^tdv-preveza]

After battle, Barbaros returned to his Preveza base with captives and prize goods.

Exact totals of ships lost, killed, wounded, captured, and prize goods remain disputed.[^tdv-preveza][^ertas-spoils]

Preveza became a major turning point in Ottoman naval policy.

But it did not settle all Mediterranean competition in one day. Castelnuovo, the Venetian settlement of 1540, North Africa, Malta, Djerba, and Lepanto all remained part of later maritime struggle.[^tdv-preveza]

This page treats Preveza as:

> A tactical galley victory, but also a Mediterranean governing event that rewired ports, islands, oarsmen, captives, prize goods, coalition diplomacy, and naval supply.

# Why This Event Matters

Preveza helps us ask:

- Why is naval battle more than artillery exchange between ships: a problem of ports, wind, currents, oarsmen, drinking water, food, repair, islands, and fortresses?
- Why could a fleet with many large sailing vessels fail to preserve automatic superiority in shallow water and along coasts?
- Why could a Holy League gather many ships but still struggle to act as one strategy?
- How did Venice, Genoa, papal power, Habsburg-Spanish power, and Malta hold different interests?
- Why did Barbaros require sailors, oarsmen, ports, soldiers, and revenue drawn from Anatolia, Egypt, islands, and North African connections?
- Why were prize goods and captives more than symbols of victory?
- Why did Preveza not end Mediterranean competition, but instead reallocate it toward Castelnuovo, Djerba, Malta, and Lepanto?

# Source Problem

## The Story of a “Miracle of the Smaller Fleet”

Preveza is often presented as a smaller Ottoman fleet defeating an overwhelmingly larger allied armada.

Exact fleet totals do not agree.

The TDV article gives one high allied total, including 140 large sailing vessels and 168 galleys, while other records classify ships and totals differently.[^tdv-preveza]

The key issue is not numbers alone, but:

- Difference between galleys and sailing vessels
- Draft and shallow water
- Wind and calm
- Artillery position
- Ports and supply
- Command structure
- Coalition politics
- Coastal-fort support

## Andrea Doria’s “Inactivity”

Some later narratives portray Doria as avoiding decisive action because of private Genoese interest or hostility toward Venice.

But ship type, wind, shallow water, risk around landing troops, preservation of fleet, and difficult coalition command must also be considered.

This page does not reduce the event to:

> Why did Doria refuse to fight a winnable battle?

## Ottoman *Gazavât* Narrative

A major source for Barbaros is Murâdî’s *Gazavât-ı Hayreddin Paşa*.

It is highly valuable for naval action and Ottoman policy, while also being a literary and political narrative centered on Barbaros’s victories and legitimacy.[^gazavat-hayreddin]

# Historical Context

## Before: Corfu, Islands, and Coalition at Sea

In 1537, Süleyman and Barbaros undertook combined land-sea activity around Apulia and Corfu.

Corfu was not decisively occupied, but Barbaros then operated in the Cyclades, Sporades, and around the Duchy of Naxos.[^tdv-preveza]

For Venice, this was not simply territorial loss.

It threatened:

- Ports
- Cargo
- Customs
- Island revenue
- Sailors
- Naval bases
- Adriatic-Aegean safety
- Levant commerce

The Holy League of 1538 was a collective answer to this reorganization of maritime circuits.

## During: Gulf, Fort, Ship Type, and Supply

At Preveza, shallow approaches around the Gulf of Narda and the fortress mattered.

Large sailing ships could carry heavier guns, but their movement could be constrained by shallow water, narrow approaches, and uncertain winds.

Galleys could move with oars and operate closer to coasts and shallow water.[^tdv-preveza][^guilmartin]

Yet galleys were not automatically superior.

They required:

- Oarsmen
- Drinking water
- Food
- Hull repair
- Powder
- Shot
- Harbors
- Landing points
- Care for sick and wounded
- Night-navigation knowledge
- Understanding of wind and current

## After: Maritime Competition Did Not End

After Preveza, Doria’s side took Castelnuovo.

The Ottoman side recaptured it in 1539 with a large fleet.[^tdv-preveza]

This shows that Preveza did not complete control of the sea in one day.

Instead, it shifted terms of competition around:

- Ottoman operational range
- Negotiation with Venice
- Difficulty of allied coordination
- Reallocation of islands and ports
- Connections among North Africa, Ionian waters, and Adriatic coasts
- The settlement of 1540

Preveza required far more than ships and guns:

- Galleys
- Sailing vessels
- Oarsmen
- Sailors
- Soldiers
- Drinking water
- Food
- Salted provisions
- Powder
- Shot
- Timber
- Canvas
- Rope
- Harbors

Naval power was therefore not only number of ships.

> Capacity to move, feed, repair, fight, and return ships to use after battle.

## Prize Goods and Captives

Prize goods and captives were not peripheral aftereffects.

They involved:

- Cargo
- Money
- Weapons
- Guns
- Enslavement
- Ransom
- Shortage of sailors
- Reports to court
- Port markets
- Family separation

Prize records show the connection of war to state finance and port economy, but must not reduce individual captivity to numbers.[^ertas-spoils]

## Navies Operate through Ports and Personnel

Ottoman naval strength after Preveza could not be sustained through individual heroism alone.

## The 1540 Settlement as Institutional Rearrangement

The Venetian settlement of 1540 documented a postwar relationship.

It did not end maritime competition.

It was a method of temporarily institutionalizing:

> Islands, ports, commerce, tribute, and zones of military operation.

The central question is not only:

> Which fleet was stronger?

> Who could connect ports, islands, oarsmen, vessels, artillery, forts, supply, and diplomacy into sustainable maritime operation?

## Reading the GMM

Preveza does not merely show that the Ottoman fleet was “strong.”

It can be read more carefully as:

> An event in which Ottoman government connected galleys, islands, ports, oarsmen, coastal forts, prize goods, naval personnel, and diplomacy more tightly, increasing maritime processing capacity from the eastern Mediterranean toward the Ionian Sea.

This capacity also involved captivity, requisition, plunder, burden on island communities, and sustained competition with rivals.

# Competing Interpretations

## Interpretation A: Advantage of Galleys in Coastal and Shallow Water

This reading emphasizes galley maneuverability, shallow approaches, calm wind, coastal forts, and artillery position.

It matters, but galleys required oarsmen, water, ports, repair, and supply.

## Interpretation B: Command and Coordination Problems in the Holy League

This reading emphasizes differing interests of Venice, Genoa, Habsburg-Spanish power, papacy, and Malta.

It asks about coalition coordination rather than reducing defeat to Doria alone.

## Interpretation C: Reorganization of Ports, Islands, and Supply

This reading treats Preveza as more than one date, looking at 1537–1540 through islands, ports, routes, oarsmen, requisition, and treaty.

## Interpretation D: Long-Term Ottoman Naval Turning Point

This reading sees Preveza as an important advantage in maritime competition from the late 1530s to before Lepanto.

It does not treat that advantage as permanent, absolute control of the whole Mediterranean.

## Sana’s Structural Hypothesis — Not Established Historical Fact

The largest question at Preveza may not have been only:

> “Which fleet had more ships?”

It may also have been:

- Which side could maintain water, food, oarsmen, ports, repair sites, and ammunition for galleys over distance?
- Which fleet could turn shallow gulf waters, coastal forts, wind, and night movement into favorable operating conditions?
- How far could a multi-state coalition combine different finances, ship types, chains of command, and political interests into one tactical plan?
- Which side could connect information and material drawn from islands, coastal communities, merchants, and sailors into naval operation?
- How could prize goods and captives be inserted into circuits of finance, labor, port market, and political communication?
- How could battle, fortress repair, and treaty be converted into durable maritime operation after the guns fell silent?

Under this view, Preveza was not only “a naval victory over a larger armada.”

It may be understood as:

> A test of which political order could better connect Mediterranean ports, islands, crews, oarsmen, forts, diplomacy, and supply into a continuing maritime system.

This does not excuse plunder, captivity, enslavement, requisition, religious hostility, or burden on coastal communities.

This is a structural hypothesis, not established historical fact.

# What This Page Does Not Claim

- It does not present Preveza only as a miracle of a smaller fleet defeating a larger fleet.
- It does not fix exact totals of ships, troops, guns, losses, or captives.
- It does not explain victory through Barbaros alone.
- It does not explain defeat through Doria’s cowardice, treachery, or self-interest alone.
- It does not claim galleys were always superior to sailing vessels.
- It does not treat the Holy League as a single actor with one interest and one command logic.
- It does not claim Preveza ended all Mediterranean competition.
- It does not treat prize goods or captives as abstract numbers of victory.
- It does not treat islanders, merchants, sailors, oarsmen, and captives as having one uniform experience.
- It does not use structural explanation to excuse naval war, plunder, captivity, enslavement, requisition, or domination.
- It does not treat Sana’s Structural Hypothesis as verified historical fact.
