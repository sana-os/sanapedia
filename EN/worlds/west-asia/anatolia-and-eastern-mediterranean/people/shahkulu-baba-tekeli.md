# Şahkulu Baba Tekeli

> **Research Status:** Provisional  
> **Central Question:** Why did the uprising led by Şahkulu in 1511 become a major political crisis connecting religious networks, Turkmen groups in Teke-ili, princely succession conflict, local military actors, towns and markets, and Safavid-linked movement?  
>
> **Note:** This page does not reduce the uprising to religious fanaticism. It also does not use local hardship to excuse looting, killing, arson, or coercion. It asks how religion, politics, mobility, military force, revenue, and succession anxiety overlapped.

---

# Human Overview

Şahkulu Baba Tekeli led a major armed uprising centered in Teke-ili in 1511. Ottoman sources also use names including Şeytankulu, Sofu, and Karabıyıkoğlu. Such terms may include the perspective of writers trying to deny the rebel leader religious and political legitimacy.[^tdv-shahkulu]

TDV identifies Şahkulu as the son of Hasan Halife, who is associated with a lodge near Yalınlıköy outside Antalya. Şahkulu appears within Turkmen religious networks connected to the Safaviyya order.[^tdv-shahkulu]

It is not established that Shah Ismail I directly ordered him to rebel.

Around 1510, however, succession conflict among Bayezid II’s sons—Ahmed, Korkut, and Selim—had made the political environment uncertain. This instability affected the relationship between central decision, local command, military actors, and provincial society.

In this environment, Şahkulu gathered Turkmen groups with Kızılbaş tendencies, former Karaman-linked military figures, and other regional actors. The uprising expanded across Teke-ili and beyond.[^tdv-shahkulu]

In spring 1511, Şahkulu’s forces moved through the Antalya region, İstanoz, Elmalı, Burdur, Hamid-ili, and toward Kütahya.

The movement included armed clashes, looting, killings, and arson. It defeated several Ottoman forces and, according to records, captured and executed Karagöz Ahmed Pasha, the beylerbeyi of Anatolia.[^tdv-shahkulu]

Records also state that Şahkulu assigned titles such as beylerbeyi, sanjak governor, and subaşı to his followers.

This may indicate that the movement was not simply mobile violence. It may have attempted to use familiar Ottoman political language to make an alternative order visible.

Bayezid II sent Atik Ali Pasha and Prince Ahmed against the uprising.

A major battle occurred near Sivas at a place identified as Çubuk or Gökçay, conventionally dated 2 July 1511. Atik Ali Pasha died in the fighting.

Şahkulu’s own fate remains unclear. Sources differ between death in battle, death after wounding, escape toward Safavid territory, or death during the later flight.[^tdv-shahkulu]

This page treats Şahkulu as:

> A rebel leader who attempted to connect religious charisma, local networks, military mobility, and an alternative language of authority within a political space destabilized by dynastic succession conflict.

# Why This Person Matters

Şahkulu Baba Tekeli helps us ask:

- Why can princely succession conflict amplify religious, social, and military revolt in the provinces?
- How can religious networks become circuits of information, mobility, support, and armed action?
- Should Safavid connection be understood as command, belief, movement, symbol, or regional politics?
- What do terms such as heretic and servant of the devil record—and what do they conceal?
- What does the use of Ottoman administrative titles by rebels suggest about alternative authority?
- How were Teke-ili, Hamid-ili, Karaman, and inner Anatolia connected through mountain routes, towns, markets, and military movement?
- What disappears when the uprising is explained only through religion, poverty, taxation, or outside manipulation?
- Why did the crisis of 1511 matter for Selim I’s accession and the path toward Chaldiran?

# Source Problem

## Names Carry Evaluation

Ottoman records sometimes call Şahkulu “Şeytankulu.”

This is not a neutral name.

It is an evaluative label used to deny the rebel leader religious and political legitimacy.

The same person appears under several names:

- Şahkulu
- Şah Kulu
- Karabıyıkoğlu
- Sofu
- Şeytankulu

Names are therefore not only search labels. They also show what record-makers regarded as dangerous.

## Do Not Separate Religion, Politics, and Local Society Too Completely

Calling the uprising only a Safavid religious rebellion can hide:

- Princely succession conflict
- Local military actors
- Teke and Hamid regional relations
- Towns, markets, and military movement

Calling it only a social or economic revolt can hide:

- Religious authority
- Kızılbaş-linked networks
- Safavid symbolism
- Messianic language
- The political function of religious affiliation

This page does not fix one cause as sufficient.

# Historical Context

## Before: Several Instabilities at the End of Bayezid II’s Reign

The 1511 uprising did not emerge from an empty setting.

Its context included:

- Bayezid II’s age and declining health
- Competition among Ahmed, Korkut, and Selim
- Religious, kinship, and mobile networks in Teke-ili
- Contacts between Safaviyya and Anatolian followers
- Local military figures and former Karaman-linked groups
- Movement of people and information across towns, mountains, roads, and ports

These should not be compressed into one cause.

The uprising may have expanded because several strains intersected at the same time, in the same region, across the same routes.

## During: From Teke to Inner Anatolia

Şahkulu’s forces moved from Teke through Hamid-ili, Kütahya-facing terrain, Alaşehir, and Karaman-facing routes.

This was not merely movement on a map.

It involved:

- Villages and towns
- Mountain paths and passes
- Markets and roads
- Soldiers and horses
- Loot, supply, and redistribution
- Local officials and forts
- Princely households
- Religious networks
- Information and rumor

Expansion depended not only on growing support, but also on Ottoman local defeats, princely uncertainty, delayed decisions, and changing local choices.

## After: From 1511 toward 1514

Şahkulu’s uprising became one background condition for Selim I’s accession in 1512 and later Safavid policy.

It should not be treated as the only direct cause of Chaldiran in 1514.

A more careful view is that the uprising contributed to an environment of:

- Anxiety over Kızılbaş-linked networks in Anatolia
- Dynastic crisis
- Eastern frontier security
- Ottoman-Safavid tension
- Movement, pursuit, and punishment after rebellion

# Layer A — Survival

## What Was Needed to Sustain an Uprising?

Rebel movement cannot be sustained by ideology alone.

It requires:

- People
- Horses
- Food
- Water
- Weapons
- Roads and passes
- Contact with villages and towns
- Material from markets
- Information
- Places of concealment
- Routes for departure and reunion
- Loot, redistribution, and supply after battle

Movement from Teke toward inner Anatolia required the ability to use mountain terrain, passes, markets, and towns.

Central forces had larger troop resources, artillery, supply, and command structures, but also faced distance, fatigue, uneven local information, cooperation problems, and terrain.

## How to Read Messianic Language

Ottoman reports state that Şahkulu declared himself Mahdi and that followers regarded him as a savior, prophet, or divinely charged figure.[^tdv-shahkulu]

This may show political-religious language circulating among parts of the movement.

But such reports also come through documents produced by authorities framing rebels as heretical threats.

What can be stated more carefully is:

> Such language was recorded and used to communicate the perceived danger of the movement.

It does not prove that every participant held the same belief in the same way.

# Layer C — Institution and Operation

## A Rebellion Using Familiar Administrative Vocabulary

Reports that Şahkulu assigned titles of beylerbeyi, sanjak governor, and subaşı matter.[^tdv-shahkulu]

This suggests the movement may have done more than reject Ottoman order.

It may have used familiar political vocabulary to make another order visible.

This does not establish durable state-building.

We do not know how long this administration lasted, where it functioned, or how widely it was obeyed.

## How Succession Crisis Affected Provincial Rule

At the end of Bayezid II’s reign, princes competed for bases, military supporters, and central recognition.

In such conditions, uncertainty could arise over:

- Who issued valid command
- Which future ruler local officials should support
- Which prince military actors would favor
- Who would prioritize response to crisis

The Şahkulu uprising can therefore be read as a case where another decision center emerged in provincial space amid competing dynastic authority.

## Central RSM Question

The central question is not only:

> How many people joined the revolt?

It is:

> When dynastic succession conflict destabilizes provincial rule, how can religious networks, regional military actors, routes of movement, towns, markets, and familiar administrative language create another center of decision?

## Reading the GMM

The problem of 1511 was not the absence of central government.

It was more precisely:

> Central command, princely competition, local military networks, and religious-political claims were presenting competing forms of legitimacy in the same regions, intensifying one another’s pressures.

# Competing Interpretations

## Interpretation A: Uprising through Safavid-Linked Religious Networks

This interpretation emphasizes Safaviyya, Kızılbaş tendencies, Shah Ismail, and language of salvation.

It does not establish direct command by Shah Ismail.

## Interpretation B: Provincial Rebellion in a Dynastic Succession Crisis

This interpretation centers on the rivalry among Bayezid II’s sons, Korkut’s movement, Ahmed’s difficulty in suppression, and Selim’s rise.

Here the rebellion expanded during a period of unstable central decision.

## Interpretation C: Coalition of Turkmen and Local Military Actors

This interpretation emphasizes Turkmen networks in Teke, former Karaman-linked fighters, and regional relations.

Religion remains important but does not exhaust the social and military composition of the movement.

## Interpretation D: Attempt at Alternative Political Order

This interpretation emphasizes assignment of office titles, communication to followers, salvation language, and use of existing administrative vocabulary.

The evidence does not establish a stable alternative state.

## Sana’s Structural Hypothesis — Not Established Historical Fact

Şahkulu’s largest problem may not have been only:

> “Can Ottoman troops be defeated?”

It may also have been:

- How can a moment of unstable command during Bayezid II’s succession crisis be used?
- How can religious and kin networks in Teke become circuits of information, movement, supply, and military participation?
- How can mountain routes, passes, markets, and towns be used to avoid central pursuit?
- How can familiar administrative titles make a rebel movement appear as more than a mobile raiding force?
- How can a relationship with Shah Ismail operate through belief, symbol, movement, and expectation rather than a direct chain of military command?
- How can a movement handle the burdens imposed by its own looting and violence on towns, supporters, and local communities?

Under this view, the uprising of 1511 was not only a “religious revolt.”

It may be understood as:

> A governing crisis in which dynastic uncertainty, local military and social networks, religious-deliverance language, roads and markets, and alternative political vocabulary briefly converged.

This does not excuse looting, killing, arson, detention, religious persecution, or violence against communities.

This is a structural hypothesis, not established historical fact.

# What This Page Does Not Claim

- It does not claim Şahkulu received direct orders from Shah Ismail I.
- It does not reduce the uprising to religion, taxation, nomadic policy, or princely succession alone.
- It does not claim that all Turkmen, Kızılbaş groups, or local military actors in Teke supported the uprising.
- It does not treat messianic, prophetic, or divinity claims as unified beliefs shared equally by all participants.
- It does not treat use of office titles as proof of a stable rebel state.
- It does not present Şahkulu only as a local social reformer.
- It does not excuse looting, killing, arson, or destruction as a result of hardship.
- It does not treat Ottoman descriptions of heresy as transparent accounts of every rebel experience.
- It does not treat the uprising as the sole direct cause of Chaldiran.
- It does not fix Şahkulu’s death as gunshot, arrow, battlefield death, or death while fleeing.
- It does not treat Sana’s Structural Hypothesis as verified historical fact.
