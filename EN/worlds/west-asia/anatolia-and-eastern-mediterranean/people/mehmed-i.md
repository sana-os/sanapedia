# Mehmed I

> **Research Status:** Provisional  
> **Central Question:** After the Battle of Ankara in 1402 and the fragmentation that followed, how far did Mehmed I reconnect towns, military actors, land and revenue, regional rulers, diplomacy, ports, and dynastic legitimacy into a functioning Ottoman government?  
>
> **Note:** This page does not treat Mehmed I simply as the “winner of the Interregnum” or as a lone reformer who restored a finished state. Victory in 1413 was a beginning. Afterwards, towns, military actors, regional houses, the Eastern Roman Empire—later conventionally called the Byzantine Empire—Venice, Serbia, Wallachia, and Timurid power still had to be dealt with as changing conditions of government.

---

# Human Overview

Mehmed I was a son of Bayezid I and ruled from 1413 to 1421 after the Ottoman Interregnum that followed the Battle of Ankara.

Later Ottoman narratives often describe him as the ruler who reunited the state.

There is a historical basis for this image.

After defeating Musa Çelebi in July 1413, Mehmed acted in Edirne as the Ottoman dynasty's sole ruler.[^tdv-mehmed][^tdv-fetret]

But 1413 was not the year in which government simply returned to its earlier condition.

During the eleven years after Ankara, Ottoman princes, Anatolian principalities, Rumelian military actors, the Eastern Roman government, Serbia, Wallachia, Venice, Genoa, and Timurid political pressure had changed the conditions of rule in different regions.

Mehmed inherited more than an empty throne.

He inherited:

- Towns including Amasya, Tokat, Bursa, and Edirne
- Military reward, land, and revenue
- Choices of autonomy, alliance, and submission among regional dynasties
- Ports, straits, ships, merchants, and customs
- Forts, roads, rural supply, and communications
- Relations with the Eastern Roman government, Serbia, Wallachia, Venice, and Genoa
- Competing dynastic claimants
- Jurists, scribes, religious institutions, and waqf
- Memories of war and civil conflict

This page treats Mehmed I as:

> A ruler attempting to reconnect the practical relations of government after civil war, not as a figure who automatically restored every earlier connection.

# Why This Person Matters

Mehmed I helps us ask:

- Does victory in civil war automatically reconnect government?
- How did an Amasya-based prince link Bursa, Edirne, Rumelia, Anatolian principalities, ports, and straits into one governing relationship?
- What did “reunification” mean differently for urban residents, rural communities, military actors, regional rulers, merchants, and diplomatic powers?
- Why did Eastern Rome, Serbia, Wallachia, and Venice support, negotiate with, or resist Mehmed at different moments?
- How did land, revenue, timar, military reward, courts, and officeholding reorganize relations between central government and regional society?
- Why do the naval conflict of 1416, Mustafa Çelebi, Cüneyd Bey, and the Bedreddin movement show that instability continued after 1413?
- How did architecture, waqf, and urban patronage make continuity of rule visible in Bursa and Edirne?

# Source Problem

## The Image of the “Reunifier”

Mehmed I is often described as the reunifier who ended the Interregnum.

This is useful for marking the political shift of 1413.

But it can hide important questions:

- Which towns accepted renewed rule, when, and under what conditions?
- Which military actors changed support because of pay, land, office, safety, or opportunity?
- Which regional dynasties chose alliance, autonomy, or submission?
- Which ports and straits shaped movement, commerce, and military logistics?
- How did Eastern Roman and Balkan rulers use rival Ottoman princes as diplomatic resources?

This page therefore treats “reunification” not as a finished result, but as:

> An attempt to make fragmented relations of government function repeatedly again.

## Victor-Centered Narrative and Regional Choice

Many Ottoman narratives were compiled under the dynasty that continued through Mehmed’s line.

This can make his victory appear natural and inevitable.

But after Ankara, towns, military groups, regional rulers, merchants, religious institutions, and outside powers acted according to their own conditions.

They were not merely background to Mehmed’s victory.

Their cooperation, neutrality, defection, negotiation, and resistance shaped whether his government could continue.

## What Can Be Stated with Confidence

### Fact: Minimum Examineable Claims

- Mehmed was born in 1386 or 1387 and was a son of Bayezid I.[^tdv-mehmed]
- In June 1399, he was reportedly appointed to govern the Rum province, including Amasya, Tokat, Sivas, and Ankara.[^tdv-mehmed]
- After Ankara in 1402, Mehmed operated mainly through Tokat and Amasya while negotiating with local military actors, regional dynasties, towns, and Timurid pressure in Anatolia.[^tdv-mehmed]
- He is associated with agreements that recognized some property and revenue rights of local actors in exchange for political support.[^tdv-mehmed]
- In 1409, Mehmed helped send Musa Çelebi into Rumelia through Wallachia in order to pressure Süleyman’s Rumelian government.[^tdv-fetret]
- On 5 July 1413, Mehmed defeated Musa at Çamurlu and subsequently acted in Edirne as sole Ottoman ruler.[^tdv-mehmed][^tdv-fetret]
- In 1413, Bayezid Pasha was appointed to high vizierial office and as beylerbeyi of Rumelia.[^tdv-bayezid-pasha]
- In 1414, Mehmed intervened in western Anatolian political-military competition around Cüneyd Bey and is associated with the reorganization of Aydın as an Ottoman sanjak.[^tdv-mehmed]
- In 1415, he campaigned against Karaman. A settlement is associated with the incorporation of Hamid-ili and parts of Said-ili into Ottoman lands.[^tdv-mehmed]
- On 29 May 1416, the Ottoman fleet suffered major losses against Venice off Gallipoli.[^tdv-mehmed]
- In 1416, Mehmed's government had to deal simultaneously with rival claims around Mustafa Çelebi and Cüneyd Bey, the movement associated with Sheikh Bedreddin, and tensions with Wallachia.[^tdv-mehmed]
- In 1419, Mehmed campaigned toward Wallachia and is associated with fortification on the right bank of the Danube.[^tdv-mehmed]
- The Bursa Green Complex was built from 1419 during Mehmed’s reign, with completion continuing around the time of his death.[^tdv-mehmed][^met-green]
- Eski Cami in Edirne was begun under Süleyman, continued under Musa, and completed under Mehmed in 1413.[^tdv-edirne]
- Mehmed died in Edirne on 25 June 1421.[^tdv-mehmed]

# Historical Context

## Before: Amasya and a Fragmented Political Space

Mehmed had been associated with the Rum province—Amasya, Tokat, Sivas, and Ankara—since 1399.

This region was not simply a peripheral refuge.

It involved:

- Turkmen and Tatar military actors
- Markets and scribal practice in towns
- Rural production and land revenue
- Regional households
- Roads, passes, and communications
- Links across Black Sea and central Anatolia
- Jurists and religious institutions

After Ankara, Mehmed used these relationships as a base for dynastic claim.

But he was not an unlimited ruler there.

Regional rulers, military groups, and urban actors acted through their own conditions of land, revenue, safety, honor, and survival.

## During: 1413 as the Start of Reconnection

After defeating Musa in 1413, Mehmed attempted to reconnect Anatolia and Rumelia under one dynasty.

Victory did not automatically restore:

- Loyalty of military actors
- Garrison security
- Distribution of land and revenue
- Passage through ports and straits
- Safety of urban markets
- Relations with regional dynasties
- Diplomacy with Eastern Rome, Serbia, and Wallachia
- Management of rival dynastic claimants
- Courts, scribes, jurists, and institutional routine

Mehmed’s main task was to translate victory into repeatable government.

## After: Crisis Continued after Reunification

Western Anatolia after 1414, Karaman in 1415, naval conflict and dynastic competition in 1416, the movement associated with Bedreddin, and campaigning toward Wallachia in 1419 all show that post-1413 rule was not settled.

This was not simply a process of restoring an old state.

It was:

> A process of reconnecting towns, regional societies, military actors, and diplomacy after they had changed through war and civil conflict.

# Layer A — Survival

## Towns, Military Actors, Countryside, Ports, and Straits

The government Mehmed tried to reconnect depended on material conditions:

- Markets and food in Amasya, Tokat, Bursa, and Edirne
- Grain, water, livestock, and revenue from rural communities
- Pay, land, revenue, booty, and office for military actors
- Forts, roads, rivers, crossings, envoys, and information
- Gallipoli, Marmara, Aegean ports, ships, and commerce
- Relations with Eastern Rome, Venice, and Genoa
- Movement between Anatolia and Rumelia
- Urban repair, religious institutions, waqf, labor, and building materials

No ruler in 1413 could control all of these fully or immediately.

Mehmed’s government had to use military activity, settlement, appointment, gift exchange, recognition of rights, endowment, and diplomacy differently in each region.

## What the Naval Defeat of 1416 Shows

The naval defeat off Gallipoli in 1416 shows that reconnection after civil war did not depend on land forces alone.

Straits and ports shaped:

- Movement between Anatolia and Rumelia
- Military transport
- Trade and customs
- Relations with Venice
- Eastern Roman diplomacy
- Safety of islands and coastal communities

The defeat did not mean total collapse.

It showed that a reunited dynasty still depended on material and diplomatic conditions it could not control unilaterally.

# Layer B — Meaning and Perception

## “Reunification” Meant Different Things to Different Communities

Mehmed’s sole rule did not mean the same thing to everyone.

| Actor | What Reconnection May Have Meant |
|---|---|
| Dynasty and advisers | Limiting rival claims, succession order, visible sole rule |
| Military actors | More predictable reward, land, office, campaigns, or stronger central scrutiny |
| Urban residents | Market and route recovery, more predictable taxation, less raiding, or new extraction |
| Rural communities | Reduced fighting, confirmation of land relations, reorganization of tax and service |
| Anatolian principalities | Survival through settlement, loss of regional space, renewed contest over former lands |
| Eastern Roman government | Renewed Ottoman power to restrain, while exiles and straits remained diplomatic resources |
| Serbia and Wallachia | New military pressure and the end of a period when several Ottoman princes could be balanced |
| Venice | Commercial stability, port security, and concern over Ottoman naval rebuilding |
| Later dynastic history | Construction of a narrative of restored order |

## Treaty and Constraint

Mehmed did not respond identically to every external pressure.

At different times he used:

- Campaign
- Settlement
- Envoy
- Gift
- Recognition of local authority
- Appointment
- Restraint
- Diplomatic accommodation

This cannot be reduced to a simple choice between strength and weakness.

After civil war, government had to:

- Gain time
- Protect towns
- Sustain military reward
- Avoid creating too many enemies at once
- Reduce opportunities for external powers to use rival Ottoman claimants

These were conditions for keeping government connected.

---

# Layer C — Institution and Operation

## Land, Revenue, and Military Actors

Land rights, revenue, timar, military reward, and local office were central to Mehmed’s government.

But these should not be described as institutions created solely by Mehmed.

During the Interregnum, regional dynasties, military groups, towns, and rural societies had related to land, revenue, and military service in varied ways.

Reconnection after 1413 required repeated choices:

- Which rights would be recognized?
- Who would receive military reward?
- Which areas would become direct sanjaks?
- Which commanders and local officials would remain in office?
- Which households would be settled with, and which would face campaign?

## Bayezid Pasha and Division of Roles

Bayezid Pasha had served Mehmed from his Amasya period and in 1413 is associated with high vizierial service and the beylerbeyilik of Rumelia.[^tdv-bayezid-pasha]

This shows that Mehmed’s government did not operate through one ruler alone.

It depended on:

- The ruler as final decision-maker
- Viziers
- Beylerbeyis
- Kadis and scribes
- Fortress commanders
- Regional military actors
- Envoys
- Port and market communities
- Waqf managers

## Architecture, Waqf, and Urban Visibility

The Green Complex in Bursa is often treated as a symbol of Mehmed’s rule.

But it was more than a monument of glory.

A complex connected:

- Worship
- Food provision
- Education
- Tomb and memorial practice
- Revenue and waqf
- Artisans and building material
- Town markets
- Dynastic memory

It should therefore be read not as proof that the state was simply restored, but as:

> An effort to make continuity of government visible in everyday urban space through endowment, labor, revenue, and dynastic memory.

## Central RSM Question

The central question is not only:

> Did Mehmed defeat his brothers?

It is:

> After victory, who could make towns, military actors, land and revenue, ports, diplomacy, and regional society function again as one governing connection?

Mehmed demonstrated sole rule.

But durable government depended on distributed roles and conditional cooperation across many regions.

## Reading the GMM

Mehmed I’s reign does not show a finished centralized state.

It more cautiously shows:

> A process of making fragmented postwar relations among towns, military actors, land and revenue, ports, diplomacy, and dynastic claim function again as a political connection.

This may have increased continuity and governing capacity.

It could also produce new burdens through land redistribution, taxation, military service, punishment, reduced regional autonomy, and inequality.

# Competing Interpretations

## Interpretation A: The Reunifier Who Ended the Interregnum

This interpretation emphasizes Mehmed’s defeat of Musa in 1413 and the reconnection of Anatolia and Rumelia under one dynasty.

It highlights dynastic continuity, military stabilization, officeholding, and urban patronage.

## Interpretation B: A Reconnector through Regional Negotiation

This interpretation emphasizes Mehmed’s regional work with military actors, towns, principalities, Eastern Rome, Serbia, Wallachia, and Venice.

Campaign, treaty, appointment, endowment, recognition of rights, and settlement become different instruments of one governing task.

## Interpretation C: A Ruler Who Rebuilt Capacity while Creating New Friction

This interpretation sees Mehmed’s government as strengthening connections among military activity, land and revenue, courts, ports, and towns.

At the same time, it could create tension for principalities, military actors, rural communities, and urban residents around tax, land rights, service, and central oversight.

## Interpretation D: Later Dynastic Memory Completed the Image of Recovery

This interpretation argues that the contrast between “Interregnum disorder” and “Mehmed’s restoration” was sharpened by later dynastic history.

It does not deny Mehmed’s political capacity.

It asks how post-1413 insecurity remained visible outside the story of victory.

## Sana’s Structural Hypothesis — Not Established Historical Fact

Mehmed I’s greatest challenge may not have been only:

> “Can he defeat Musa?”

After 1413, he may also have needed to answer:

- Can Amasya and Edirne operate within one government?
- Can Bursa’s markets and dynastic memory be secured?
- Can military actors receive reward and rank?
- Can regional principalities find reasons to cooperate rather than pursue full autonomy?
- Can he manage war and settlement at the same time with Eastern Rome, Serbia, Wallachia, and Venice?
- Can ports, ships, straits, and commerce remain usable?
- Can land, revenue, court, and office be reorganized in ways communities accept?
- Can building and waqf make continuity visible in urban life?

Mehmed I’s “reunification” may therefore have been not only:

> A dynasty ruling again,

but also:

> A process of making severed relations among towns, military actors, land, ports, diplomacy, and dynastic memory operate repeatedly again.

This reconnection may not have benefited all communities in the same way.

It could involve taxation, military service, redistribution of revenue, punishment, military pressure, and inequality.

This is a structural hypothesis, not established historical fact.

# What This Page Does Not Claim

- It does not claim that Mehmed’s birth year, maternal background, or childhood are fully established.
- It does not claim that victory in 1413 immediately restored uniform Ottoman government across Anatolia, Rumelia, and the Balkans.
- It does not claim that Mehmed personally created or completed timar, survey, land-right, and revenue institutions by himself.
- It does not treat every Anatolian principality as simply obedient or simply resistant.
- It does not treat relations with Eastern Rome, Serbia, Wallachia, and Venice as permanently allied or permanently hostile.
- It does not use the naval defeat of 1416 as total proof of Ottoman naval weakness or failure of Mehmed’s government.
- It does not reduce the Bedreddin movement of 1416 to one religious, social, or political cause.
- It does not treat the Green Complex as only propaganda or as automatic proof of social welfare.
- It does not use “reunification” to erase the burdens of land right, military service, taxation, war, or regional autonomy.
- It does not use structural explanation to excuse war, punishment, requisition, taxation, inequality, or domination.
- It does not treat Sana’s Structural Hypothesis as verified historical fact.
