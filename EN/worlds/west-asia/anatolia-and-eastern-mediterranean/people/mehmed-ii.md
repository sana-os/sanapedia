# Mehmed II

> **Research Status:** Provisional  
> **Central Question:** Through early dynastic crisis, the fall of Constantinople in 1453, and subsequent reorganization of city, population, ports, property, religious communities, elite households, and military resources, how did Mehmed II attempt to remake Ottoman government into a more wide-ranging imperial political order?  
>
> **Note:** This page does not treat Mehmed II merely as “the Conqueror” or as a modern state-builder who designed everything alone. Conquest, resettlement, waqf, land, taxation, religious communities, military actors, ports, straits, and later memory are examined as distinct but connected layers.

---

# Human Overview

Mehmed II was born in Edirne on 30 March 1432. He ruled first from 1444 to 1446, then again from 1451 until 1481.[^tdv-mehmed]

He is strongly remembered as “the Conqueror” because Ottoman forces took Constantinople in 1453.

But 1453 did not complete government.

Taking a city and making it into a city where people could live, food could arrive, ports could operate, markets could reopen, taxation and courts could function, religious communities could continue, and military-diplomatic networks could connect were different tasks.

Mehmed inherited:

- The military, diplomatic, and vizierial balances of Murad II’s reign
- The instability of his own first reign
- Politics of the Marmara straits and the ports of the Black Sea and Aegean
- The continuing presence and diplomatic role of the Eastern Roman Empire—later conventionally called the Byzantine Empire
- Regional diversity across Rumelia, Anatolia, the Balkans, Black Sea, and Mediterranean
- Questions of pay, revenue, booty, land rights, and rank for military actors
- The daily conditions of townspeople, merchants, artisans, rural communities, and religious figures
- The post-conquest problem of property, population, office, and religious institutions

After 1453, Mehmed’s government reorganized Istanbul through repopulation, construction, waqf, trade, ports, communal institutions, administration, and military infrastructure.[^tdv-mehmed]

This reorganization also involved forced movement, taxation, coinage change, intervention in property and waqf, military service, punishment, and reduced room for some local autonomies.

This page treats Mehmed as:

> A ruler who attempted to convert military conquest into continuing imperial government through the reorganization of city, population, revenue, religious community, straits, and elite structure.

# Why This Person Matters

Mehmed II helps us ask:

- How might an early experience of losing power shape later rule?
- What is the difference between conquering a city and transforming it into a governable capital?
- Who gained opportunity and who carried the burdens of post-1453 repopulation?
- How did ports, straits, ships, merchants, rural supply, coinage, and taxation connect to imperial government?
- How did arrangements involving Orthodox, Armenian, Jewish, Muslim, and Latin communities combine protection, regulation, taxation, and communal reorganization?
- How did intervention in land, waqf, and private property affect military finance and local society?
- What did titles such as “sultan,” “khan,” and “Kayser-i Rûm” communicate to different audiences?
- Why has 1453 acquired different meanings in Ottoman, Greek Orthodox, Latin Christian, Balkan, Islamic, and modern national memories?

# Source Problem

## “The Conqueror” Is Not Enough

The record for Mehmed II is unusually rich.

It includes Ottoman chronicles, Eastern Roman narratives, Venetian and Genoese documents, Latin reports, buildings, inscriptions, coinage, waqf deeds, communal memory, literature, and portraiture.

More records do not make interpretation simple.

Mehmed appears at once as:

- Conqueror
- Ruler who ended Eastern Roman rule in Constantinople
- Claimant to Roman imperial language
- Islamic sovereign
- Urban reorganizer
- Military and fiscal reformer
- Patron of learning and art
- Intervener in land, waqf, and community arrangements

These images do not fully match.

## 1453 Included Urban Rupture and Urban Reorganization

The fall of Constantinople caused major rupture in population, institutions, property, religious practice, and urban landscape.

At the same time, Mehmed’s government needed to make Istanbul function again as a center of administration, commerce, religion, defense, and military movement.

The post-conquest city should therefore not be understood simply as:

> A destroyed city,

or as:

> A city immediately rebuilt.

It was rather:

> A city in which destruction, resettlement, movement, repair, confiscation, endowment, negotiation, and commercial reconnection took place together.

- Mehmed II was born in Edirne on 30 March 1432.[^tdv-mehmed]

- Rumeli Hisarı was constructed on the European side of the Bosporus in 1452.[^tdv-mehmed]
- The siege lasted from 6 April to 29 May 1453, when Ottoman forces took Constantinople.[^tdv-mehmed]

- Mehmed’s government was involved in the reorganization of population, commerce, ports, religious institutions, administration, military infrastructure, and property in Istanbul.[^tdv-mehmed]
- Georgios Scholarios is recorded as becoming patriarch after 1453 under the name Gennadios II.[^tdv-mehmed]
- Morea was incorporated in 1460 and Trebizond in 1461.[^tdv-mehmed]
- The Ottoman–Venetian war lasted from 1463 to 1479.[^tdv-mehmed]
- On 11 August 1473, Ottoman forces defeated Uzun Hasan’s Aq Qoyunlu army at Otlukbeli.[^tdv-otlukbeli]
- Ottoman intervention advanced around Caffa and the Crimean coast in 1475.[^tdv-mehmed]

# Historical Context

## Before: Crisis of Youthful Rule

Mehmed II’s first reign showed that formal accession did not automatically create one center of decision-making.

Between 1444 and 1446, political pressure involved:

- Murad II’s military prestige
- Administrative experience of Çandarlı Halil Pasha and other senior figures
- Janissary pay and political leverage
- Crusading and Hungarian military pressure
- Balkan and Anatolian regional rulers
- Urban and commercial security
- Rival dynastic claimants such as Orhan Çelebi

When Mehmed returned in 1451, he needed not only to rule in his father’s place but to reorganize the conditions under which authority could endure.

## During: 1453 as Conquest and Urban Reorganization

The siege involved walls, artillery, military actors, ships, straits, food, roads, timber, finance, diplomacy, and fear of external relief.

After the fall, different burdens began:

- How to increase population
- How to position property among state, private holders, and waqf
- How to reactivate ports, roads, markets, and trade
- How to deal with religious institutions and communities
- Where to place palace, fortifications, offices, courts, and scribal functions
- How to connect capital expenditure to provincial revenue
- Who would bear the financial costs of capital formation

Post-1453 Istanbul was not simply a new Ottoman city.

It was a space in which residents, buildings, commercial relations, religions, and institutions with different pasts were being rearranged.

## After: Burdens of Wide-Ranging Rule

Morea, Trebizond, Bosnia, Karaman, the Black Sea, Crimea, Venice, Uzun Hasan, and Otranto were distinct regional problems.

But for Mehmed’s government they shared practical questions:

- Military reward
- Land and revenue
- Ports and maritime movement
- Forts and roads
- Diplomacy and settlement
- Local powerholders
- Rural and urban supply
- Making sultanic sovereignty visible

# Layer A — Survival

## Material Conditions of Making a Capital

Turning post-1453 Istanbul into a governing center required more than symbolic victory.

It required:

- Food, water, fuel, and building materials
- Markets, artisans, sailors, merchants, and carriers
- Ports, ships, straits, roads, and warehouses
- Repair and defense
- Housing, occupation, and religious institutions for newcomers
- Military pay, land and revenue, office, and expected reward
- Scribes, courts, taxation, and coinage
- Waqf and continuing revenue
- Flow of grain, livestock, wood, tax, and information from wider regions

Repopulation was therefore not merely a question of numbers.

It was a question of resource organization necessary for a city to become a lived, commercial, administrative, religious, and military node again.

## Repopulation and Coercive Movement

The process included:

- Movement seeking trade or occupational opportunity
- Movement encouraged by tax and property incentives
- Organized transfer from local communities
- Movement resulting from warfare, captivity, and household disruption

Repopulation should not be presented only as successful urban revival.

For some, it could mean access to new work and markets.

For others, it could mean separation from home, land, relatives, and local networks.

## Roman, Islamic, and Dynastic Languages

Mehmed used several political idioms: sultan, padishah, khan, and Kayser-i Rûm.

This cannot be reduced to:

> Mehmed became the Eastern Roman emperor.

These titles could connect:

- Ottoman dynastic succession
- Islamic sovereignty
- Turkic-Mongol khanic language
- Istanbul’s imperial centrality
- Diplomatic claims around Roman legacy
- Communication with different Balkan, Black Sea, Mediterranean, and Anatolian audiences

---

# Layer C — Institution and Operation

## City, Patriarchate, and Community

Post-1453 communal arrangements cannot be reduced to either tolerance or oppression.

The Orthodox patriarchate could provide a framework for religious continuity.

It could also create an institutional contact point through which the Ottoman state could identify, negotiate with, tax, and regulate Orthodox communities.

The installation of Gennadios II is an important case of this double character.

Institutional recognition could mean protection.

It could also mean administrative visibility and control.

## Land, Waqf, and Revenue

Mehmed’s intervention in land, waqf, and private property connected to capital construction, military cost, timar, and reward for officeholders.

TDV reports broad measures returning land that had moved beyond direct state control into the miri domain and reallocating villages and cultivated land to timar holders.[^tdv-mehmed]

This should not be treated as one person finishing a complete system.

It involved varied regional adjustment among:

- Existing local rights
- Waqf revenue
- Jurists and religious figures
- Timar holders
- Rural communities
- Markets
- Military expenditure
- Capital building

## Fiscal Burden and Everyday Life

Capital-building and recurrent campaigning increased fiscal demand.

The period saw renewed coinage, taxation, and forms of state monopoly involving everyday goods, and sources report significant social dissatisfaction.[^tdv-mehmed]

This is not merely evidence of stronger state capacity.

It raises a different question:

> How did the cost of sustaining capital, army, port, and campaign appear in the daily lives of rural producers, merchants, artisans, and consumers?

The central question is not only:

> After conquest, who could connect city, population, port, military actor, land and revenue, religious community, and elite household into government that functioned repeatedly?

## Reading the GMM

Mehmed II’s reign does not show a finished modern state.

It more cautiously shows:

> A process of reorganizing conquest, city, finance, military force, religious community, straits, and local society into a governing relationship that could work across a wider space.

This broadened military, commercial, and administrative connection.

It also intensified burdens of transfer, taxation, property intervention, service, punishment, communal regulation, and inequality.

# Competing Interpretations

## Interpretation A: The Conqueror Who Created an Imperial Center

This interpretation emphasizes 1453, Istanbul, expanded titles, military prestige, and the wider reach of Ottoman power.

## Interpretation B: A Reorganizer of the Post-Conquest City

This interpretation sees Mehmed’s importance not only in the fall itself, but in repopulation, markets, ports, construction, waqf, communal institutions, and city administration.

## Interpretation C: A Ruler Who Increased Capacity while Deepening Burden and Friction

This interpretation emphasizes how intervention in military, finance, land, waqf, and communities made government more capable while creating pressure on rural society, urban residents, religious figures, and holders of established rights.

## Interpretation D: Later Memory Compressed Complex Rule into “the Conqueror”

This interpretation argues that the powerful later image of Fatih can obscure resettlement, taxation, communal governance, coercive movement, property intervention, and ordinary urban administration.

## Sana’s Structural Hypothesis — Not Established Historical Fact

Mehmed II’s greatest challenge may not have been only:

> “Can Constantinople be taken?”

After 1453, he may also have needed to answer:

- How can a damaged city become a city where people can live?
- How can resettled people find reasons to remain after movement?
- How can ports, ships, straits, roads, and markets operate again?
- How can reward for military actors be balanced with the cost of capital-building?
- How can Orthodox, Muslim, Armenian, Jewish, and Latin communities be placed within government?
- How can land, waqf, and private property be rearranged while managing conflict?
- How can Roman, Islamic, and Ottoman dynastic languages be used for different regions?
- How can military prestige be translated into tax, court, food supply, building, and safety?

Mehmed II’s imperial government may therefore have been not:

> A political order completed through one conquest,

but:

> A recurring effort to turn the burdens created by conquest—city, population, property, religion, military, diplomacy, and revenue—into governable connections.

This did not benefit every community in the same way.

It may have involved transfer, taxation, property reorganization, community control, military obligation, inequality, and violence.

This is a structural hypothesis, not established historical fact.

# What This Page Does Not Claim

- It does not claim that Mehmed exercised complete independent authority during his first reign.
- It does not claim that the fall of Constantinople immediately made Istanbul a stable capital.
- It does not describe repopulation as entirely voluntary or entirely forced.
- It does not reduce the position of Orthodox, Armenian, Jewish, Muslim, and Latin communities to one model of tolerance or coercion.
- It does not claim that Mehmed alone created or completed land, waqf, tax, kanun, and administrative institutions.
- It does not treat centralization as automatic progress or as a universal social benefit.
- It does not treat Kayser-i Rûm as a title that simply completed succession to Eastern Roman sovereignty.
- It does not describe post-1453 Ottoman rule as simple imitation of Eastern Roman institutions.
- It does not treat poisoning of Mehmed II as established fact.
- It does not use structural explanation to excuse conquest, siege, captivity, transfer, confiscation, taxation, punishment, inequality, or domination.
- It does not treat Sana’s Structural Hypothesis as verified historical fact.
